## Deep Research

### 1. By the Numbers — the read-ready stat sheet

*Order suggested for Cyrus's read: adoption → the plate → two fridges vs. none → U.S. vs. Africa → the paradox (emitter and preventer) → Kigali. Each entry: the exact number, the anchor, the exact hedge, the source, and the believability check.*

#### ⭐ 1a. The adoption curve — 1930 → 1940 → 1950
- **1940 (the census year, primary):** 44.1% of occupied homes reporting had a mechanical refrigerator; 27.1% an icebox; 27.4% nothing; 1.4% "other" — a well cooler or a wet-cloth evaporative frame. The census defined the question as "the principal equipment available for the refrigeration of food for the household during the summer months." (Source: 1940 Census of Housing, Vol. II, Part 1, Table 9b — **web-verified**.)
- **1930 ≈ 8% and 1950 ≈ 80%:** carried from Phase 1 (AEI; Pacific Standard; Paleofuture — secondary). `[VERIFY]` The 1950 Census of Housing also asked the refrigeration question; training data recalls ~80% mechanical — the Fact Checker should pull the 1950 table the same way (www2.census.gov, 1950 housing volumes).
- **Contradiction to know about:** the Comin–Hobijn technology-diffusion series (via Our World in Data) gives refrigerator 12.8% (1930), 63% (1940), 86.4% (1950). The 1940 gap (63 vs. 44) is large; the likely explanation is a different denominator (wired or non-farm households) or a manufacturer-shipments estimate rather than the census. (Source: OWID CSV https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/technology-adoption-by-households-in-the-united-states.csv — **web-verified**.) **Recommendation:** for 1940 use the census's 44% and say "the census"; for 1930 and 1950 say "roughly" and "about four in five."
- **Anchors (Jeff's ask), all from the same Comin–Hobijn series except TV:**
  - **Radio:** 10% (1925) → 45% (1930) → 81% (1940). Almost exactly the fridge's pace, ten years earlier. (OWID — **web-verified**.)
  - **Television:** "In 1948, 1 percent of U.S. households owned at least one television… in 1955, 75 percent did." (Source: Wikipedia, *Television in the United States*, citing its ref. 4 — **web-verified secondary**; training data adds 9% in 1950.) Faster than the fridge.
  - **Telephone:** the series crosses 50% in **1946** — seventy years after Bell's 1876 patent (34% in 1920; it *fell* to 31% in 1934 as Depression households gave up their phones). (OWID — **web-verified**.) **Correction for the script:** the overview's "fifty years to half of homes" should be "about seventy years."
  - **Electricity:** 10% (1908) → 33.5% (1920) → 68% (1929); 78% by 1940. (OWID — **web-verified**.)
- **Suggested read:** "In 1930, roughly one American home in twelve had a refrigerator. In the 1940 census it was forty-four percent — and twenty-seven percent still had an icebox. By 1950 it was about four in five. That's the pace of radio, slower than television, and it happened during the Great Depression."
- `[BELIEVABILITY]` Passes. The Depression detail makes it *more* believable, not less, because the price fell from ~$600 to ~$150 across the decade (Phase 1, AEI).

#### ⭐ 1b. Three-quarters of the plate
- Twilley (2012): "More than three-quarters of the food consumed in the United States today is processed, packaged, shipped, stored, and sold under artificial refrigeration." (Cabinet 47 — **web-verified**.) *Frostbite* (2024) restates it as "nearly three-quarters" / "three-quarters of all food that reaches plates in the U.S." (reviews — secondary). **Page cite still open** — see markers table.
- **Anchor from the same book (secondary, `[VERIFY]` against the book):** "5.5 billion cubic feet of refrigerated warehouse space in the U.S., or 16.4 cubic feet per person… the equivalent of more than 1,500 Costco warehouses." (Loudoun Wildlife review — **web-verified secondary**.) *Suggested framing:* "about one home refrigerator's worth of warehouse cold for every man, woman, and child in the country, sitting somewhere you've never been."
- `[BELIEVABILITY]` Passes on first hearing; the warehouse-per-person anchor is the one that makes people sit up.

#### ⭐ 1c. Two fridges vs. no fridge
- **U.S.:** 42.53 million of 123.53 million homes (34.4%) use two or more refrigerators; 0.66 million (0.5%) use none. (EIA RECS 2020, Table HC3.1 — **web-verified**.)
- **World:** "just over 1 billion people are at high risk of a lack of access to crucial cooling solutions" — 309 million rural poor, 695 million urban poor — across 77 countries; another 2.83 billion at medium risk. (SEforALL 2025 — **web-verified**.) Rural sub-Saharan Africa: 4% of households own a refrigerator (2020). (SEforALL — **web-verified**.)
- **Suggested read:** "One in three American homes has two refrigerators. About a billion people — roughly one in eight — are at high risk for having no cooling at all. In rural sub-Saharan Africa it's four homes in a hundred."
- `[BELIEVABILITY]` Passes. Do not say "one in seven" unless attributing to the 2022 report.

#### ⭐ 1d. The United States and Africa (the defensible form)
- IEA, p. 24: "328 million Americans consume more energy for cooling than the 4.4 billion people living in all of Africa, Latin America, the Middle East and Asia (excluding China), and just under all the electricity used for everything by the 1.2 billion people in Africa." (**web-verified**.)
- Scope line, if wanted (pick one, attribute, don't blend): IEA — "space cooling accounted for around 10% of total electricity demand" globally in 2016 (p. 27; highest shares U.S. 16%, Middle East 15%) — **web-verified**; IIR — the whole refrigeration sector "consumes about 20% of the overall electricity used worldwide" (IIR 2019 note; a 3rd edition exists as the 60th IIR Technical Brief) — **web-verified**.
- `[BELIEVABILITY]` Cyrus's "incredible if true" — it is true in this form. The two-and-a-half-times *global* form is a different sentence and sounds like an exaggeration. Say the U.S. form, say "the IEA."

#### ⭐ 1e. The paradox — emitter and preventer
- **Emitter:** cooling CO₂ "tripled since 1990 to 1 130 million tonnes (Mt), equivalent to the total emissions of Japan" (IEA 2018, p. 11 — **web-verified**). ACs alone: 1.75 Gt CO₂e = 3.2% of global GHG in 2022, of which 0.72 Gt is refrigerant leakage (OWID/Ritchie 2024 — **web-verified**). The food cold chain: "around four percent of total global greenhouse gas emissions" (FAO/UNEP 2022 — **web-verified**). All cooling ≈ 7% (UNEP 2023 — **not re-verified**, see markers).
- **Preventer:** "lack of effective refrigeration directly results in the loss of 526 million tons of food production, or 12 per cent of the global total"; those losses "totalled an estimated 1 gigatonne of carbon dioxide (CO2) equivalent in 2017 – about 2 percent of total global greenhouse gas emissions." (FAO/UNEP 2022 — **web-verified**.) Context from the same release: "The number of people affected by hunger in the world rose to 828 million in 2021."
- **Suggested read:** "The cold chain is about four percent of global emissions. The food that rots because there *isn't* a cold chain is about two percent — and it's twelve percent of everything grown."
- `[BELIEVABILITY]` Passes; the two numbers side by side are what make it land.

#### ⭐ 1f. Kigali — a treaty about refrigerants
- "Compliance with the 2016 Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol… is estimated to avoid 0.3–0.5°C of warming by 2100." (WMO/UNEP Scientific Assessment 2022 — **web-verified**.) EPA: "avoiding up to 0.5° Celsius warming by the end of the century" (**web-verified**).
- Anchor: the Montreal Protocol is "the first universally ratified treaty in United Nations history" — 198 parties (Wikipedia, universally repeated; UN Ozone Secretariat page returned 403 — **secondary**). Kofi Annan: "perhaps the single most successful international agreement to date has been the Montreal Protocol" (Wikipedia, cited — **secondary**).
- **Suggested read:** "The amendment that phases down the *replacement* for Freon is expected to avoid somewhere between a third and half a degree of warming by 2100. A treaty about what's inside your refrigerator is one of the biggest single climate actions anyone has ever taken."
- `[BELIEVABILITY]` Passes when bracketed ("a third to half a degree"); "0.4" as a point estimate sounds falsely precise.

- Roughly **5 billion** refrigeration, AC and heat-pump systems in operation; "roughly 500 billion USD" annual sales; "over 15 million people are employed worldwide in the refrigeration sector." (IIR 2019 — **web-verified**.) Anchor: one machine for every 1.6 people alive.

The **5.6 billion by 2050** figure is the IEA's news-release wording (**training data**; OWID paraphrases "over 5.5 billion"). "Ten new ACs sold every second for the next thirty years" is also news-release wording, not in the report body — `[VERIFY]` or say "by the IEA's count, something like ten a second."
- **The 1940 census inequality (for Q3, or as a By the Numbers coda):** nonwhite households in 1940 — 9.8% mechanical, 39.8% ice, **49.8% nothing**; in the South, nonwhite households — 4.6% mechanical, **60.2% nothing**. ### 3. Backbone Test Q1 — Invisible?

**Yes — and the texture is Twilley's.** All from "The Coldscape" (Cabinet 47, 2012 — **web-verified**), which is the essay *Frostbite* grew out of:

- ⭐ **The orange-juice tank farms.** "Tropicana (a Pepsico company) was the industry leader, building 250,000-gallon stainless steel, individually insulated and refrigerated storage cylinders in an outdoor 'tank farm' at its Bradenton, Florida, facility in the mid-1980s. Today, tanks typically boast capacities of a million or a million-and-a-half gallons." Coca-Cola's Simply Orange runs "a twenty-five million gallon indoor tank farm eighty miles inland at Auburndale." Combined capacity exceeds a hundred million gallons, supplying "more than one in five Americans with their daily OJ." The process: juice is pasteurized, de-oiled, stripped of oxygen, chilled to 32°F and pumped into "million-gallon, refrigerated, epoxy resin-lined, carbon steel, aseptic storage tanks," where it "often sits for as long as a year, from processing season to processing season." (A Georgia Tech supply-chain professor who visited Auburndale: "It looked like the final scene in *Raiders of the Lost Ark*, the government warehouse that just goes on forever" — Georgia Tech ISyE, **web-verified**.)

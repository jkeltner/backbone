# Chapter 02 Research: WAVE 1 — Cold as a Commodity (1806–1900)

## Chapter Context

**Blueprint summary (lines 105–187):** A mocked twenty-three-year-old spends two decades losing money, then builds — from pond water, sawdust, and a horse-drawn plow — the first global cold commodity chain, with a mechanic (Wyeth) who makes it profitable. The trade grows into a Hudson Valley industry of thousands of workers and hundreds of icehouses; it teaches cities to *expect* cold; and it becomes an incumbent that laughs a Florida doctor's ice machine out of the market in the 1850s. The wave closes with the Ice Trust doubling the price of ice in a New York heat wave and a mayor's name on the shareholder list. Two anchor stories (Roffignac 1826; the *Tuscany* at Calcutta 1833), a Gorrie foreshadow beat (~3 min), Thoreau as 30-second color, the Ice Trust as a closing set piece, and a bridge built on the 1890 ice famine and the 1890 price map. Lens map: Jeff leads Tudor, Roffignac, the Ice Trust/Tammany; Cyrus leads sawdust/insulation, Wyeth's cost curve, the incumbency read at Gorrie, the 1890 price map; Calcutta is shared via the "did you come across…" move.

**Gaps to fill (blueprint lines 561–568 plus inline markers):** national natural-ice claims ("90,000 workers," "25,000 horses," "second-largest export after cotton"); Wyeth's 30¢→10¢; a period source for Roffignac; period Calcutta sources for the blistered palm / "grows on trees" and the captain's name; Gorrie's *Globe* quote, statue, backer's name, Tudor's role; Ice Trust price move, Van Wyck's share counts, "Tammany lost the next election"/Seth Low; Bavaria's summer-brewing ban.

## Deep Research

### Narrative Arc

1. **The joke (1806–1813).** Tudor, 23, ships pond ice to Martinique; the *Boston Gazette* mocks it; the cargo mostly melts because there is no icehouse at the far end and nobody knows what to do with ice. Losses; debtors' prison (1812–13). The founder's problem is not cold — it is *melt* and *demand*.
2. **Solving melt (1810s–1820s).** Havana icehouse (1815–16, double-shelled, ~150 tons). Insulation experiments; tan bark and sawdust win because they trap dead air and cost nothing. Wyeth's plow (1825) collapses harvest cost and standardizes the block.
3. **Manufacturing demand (1820s).** Charleston (1820), New Orleans (1821), Savannah. The bartender playbook and the "cold drinks at the same price" letter. The resisting mayor who reportedly dumps a cargo in the river.
4. **The trade goes global (1833).** The *Tuscany* at Calcutta; a colonial government waives duties and lets the ice land at night; a public icehouse; ice sold at half the price of the local slush. Thoreau's pond ends up in the Ganges.
5. **The incumbent forms (1830s–1850s).** Boston's 353 ice ships in 1847; two million tons in storage by 1855; "the Ice King." A Florida doctor makes ice by machine (1844–51) and the trade — and a New York paper — laugh him into the grave.
6. **Scale (1880s–1890s).** The Hudson as an ice factory: 135 big icehouses, tens of thousands of winter jobs, New York's 3 million tons a year. The iceman's card in every window.
7. **Fragility and corruption (1890–1900).** The warm winter of 1889–90; Savannah's machine ice at half New York's price; then Charles W. Morse's American Ice Company, a doubled price in a heat wave, no more nickel pieces, and a mayor on the shareholder list. The city discovers its cold supply is both fragile and for sale.

### The Breakthrough — Tudor as founder (Jeff leads)

**The two decades of losses.** Tudor's first cargo cleared Boston February 10, 1806 for Martinique. The *Boston Gazette*: "No joke. A vessel with a cargo of 80 tons of ice has cleared out from this port for Martinique. We hope this will not prove to be a slippery speculation." (Source: [HNOC](https://hnoc.org/publishing/first-draft/the-big-freezy); [Investor Amnesia](https://investoramnesia.com/2024/02/28/breaking-the-ice/)). He lost about $4,500 — nearly the price of the brig itself ($4,750). (Source: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederic_Tudor); Investor Amnesia). `[FLAG: Opening chapter owns the 80-vs-130-ton discrepancy; note that the 1861 New American Cyclopaedia — a period source — says "a cargo of 130 tons in his own brig," so both numbers have pedigree.]` The failure was structural, not thermal: "the lack of local storage facilities" meant "ice stocks quickly melted away" on the dock, and nobody in Martinique had a use for ice. (Source: [Wikipedia, Ice trade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_trade)). Cuba lost him more than Martinique had. His first profitable year was 1810 — about $1,000. Then war, a partner's fraud, and debtors' prison in 1812–13. (Sources: Investor Amnesia; Wikipedia.)

**Family capital.** The Tudors were a Boston legal family with a farm pond; the first ice came from Rockwood in Saugus; the losses were absorbed by a family that could absorb them. (Source: Wikipedia.) ⭐ The honest founder-story detail is that Tudor's twenty years of losses were *financed*, not survived on grit — which is exactly the "Silicon Valley" comparison Cyrus made, and a fair one.

**Solving the far end: Havana.** By 1815 Tudor had borrowed $2,100 to build a double-shelled icehouse in Havana holding about 150 tons — the first piece of *destination infrastructure*. ### Wyeth and the plow — the cost curve (Cyrus leads)

**What the tool did (period descriptions).** Ice was laid out like a field. A line was staked and a hand plow scored a base groove; then a horse-drawn **marker** (a toothed sled with a swinging guide arm that rode in the previous groove) cut parallel grooves about three inches deep across the whole field; then the **plow** — "a series of small cutting chisels, one succeeding another and deepening the groove" (*New-York Tribune*, Nov 1858) — ran back and forth in the marker grooves "until the grooves have been cut about two-thirds through the ice, care being taken to leave not less than four inches of solid ice below the bottom of the groove." A large wooden square with fifteen-foot legs squared the field, and the whole thing was repeated at right angles. Blocks were then split off with bars, floated down cut channels to the icehouse, and hoisted. A plow cost $60–90; a marker about $75. (Sources: [Hiles, *The Ice Crop*, 1893, pp. 22–25](https://archive.org/details/icecrophowtoharv00hilerich); [Hudson River Maritime Museum, quoting *New-York Tribune* Nov 3/16/19, 1858](https://www.hrmm.org/history-blog/category/ice-harvesting)). Wyeth's 1825 original was a "twin-bladed, horse-drawn ice-cutter" with "iron runners about 20 inches apart." (Sources: [Scroll.in](https://scroll.in/article/720912/how-ice-shipped-all-the-way-from-america-became-a-luxury-item-in-colonial-india); *Early American Life* 1976.)

⭐ **The two effects — Cyrus's beat.** (1) *Cost:* "Wyeth's basic invention cut costs of ice harvesting from about thirty cents to ten cents a ton" (Cummings 1949) — output "more than tripled" is the same fact from the labor side (Antique Ice Tool Museum: "tripling the amount of ice harvested"). (2) *Standardization:* uniform blocks "could also be packed tightly to quell melting" (Scroll.in) and, per Shachtman, "because it produced extremely regular blocks, it made shippers more eager to" carry ice. A uniform block is a *unit* — it can be priced, stacked without air gaps (air gaps are melt), and loaded like "so many barrels of flour" (Thoreau). This is the moment ice becomes a commodity in the literal sense.
- **Anchor for the number:** a two-thirds cut in harvest cost is roughly what containerization did to port-handling costs a century later — or, simpler: the same man and horse now delivered three tons where they had delivered one. `[BELIEVABILITY]` fine.
- "Under the arrangement with Wyeth, Tudor controlled the use of all these devices" (Boorstin) — Jeff's "founder locks up the operations IP" beat.

**Hiles's own framing of the industry's growth, for scale (period voice):** "in 1825, the ice consumed in the United States and exported to foreign ports was probably less than fifty thousand tons… Two million tons is a liberal estimate of the amount of ice stored… in 1855, in the United States, with six or seven million dollars of invested capital." (Source: Hiles 1893, pp. 7–8.) That is a forty-fold increase in thirty years — from the plow to the Civil War.

### Sawdust and insulation (Cyrus)

**Why hay failed and sawdust worked.** The insulating material in an ice ship or icehouse is not the stuff — it is the *still air* trapped in the stuff. Hay and straw compress, mat, get wet and channel meltwater, and admit moving air; wet insulation conducts. Dry sawdust or spent tan bark packs into a dense mass of tiny dead-air pockets, stays granular when it gets damp, and drains. Thoreau saw the failure mode in the field: "when the wind, though never so cold, finds a passage through, it will wear large cavities, leaving slight supports or studs only here and there, and finally topple it down." (Source: *Walden*, "The Pond in Winter.") The period manual's rules of thumb: icehouse walls are hollow and "filled with non-conducting substance, usually sawdust or spent tan bark. **The filling must be dry**"; in ships, "eight inches of sawdust are placed at the sides of the vessel… [other materials] may be used, but sawdust is the best"; ice shipped any distance is "packed in sawdust and done up in burlap." (Source: [Hiles 1893](https://archive.org/details/icecrophowtoharv00hilerich)). `[FLAG for the writer: the physics claim "trapped air, low conductivity" is standard building science (training data), stated here without a fetched primary; it is safe.]`

**Free waste stream.** Sawdust was the waste of New England's sawmills; tan bark was the spent waste of tanneries. The *Tuscany*'s cargo to Calcutta "was surrounded with **tan**," and the Havana method was "dry tan." (Sources: [Asiatic Journal 1834](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.48090); *New American Cyclopaedia* 1861.) Jeff's "free markets at work" beat: one industry's disposal problem became another industry's key input at zero marginal cost. `[FLAG: I did not find a source for the specific "Maine sawmills" supply chain; say "New England sawmills."]`

**Tudor's icehouse design.** Double walls with the cavity filled (Wyeth is credited with "ice houses with double-wall insulation" — DAB via Wikipedia); "inner and outer timber walls, insulated with peat and sawdust" (Wikipedia, Ice trade); drainage so meltwater never pools against the ice; access from the roof to limit warm-air exchange (Scroll.in: storehouses "accessible from the roof, to reduce the melting"). On the *Tuscany*, the ice "was so stowed as to allow the meltings to drain off at once to the pump-well, and by constant attention to the pump, the hold was kept tolerably dry." (Source: Asiatic Journal 1834.)

**Melt losses — what can and cannot be said.** `[VERIFY]` I found **no** Tudor-specific "60% → under 10%" figure. What is sourced: "In the 1820s and 1830s only 10% of ice harvested was eventually sold to the end user due to wastage en route… by the end of the 19th century… wastage in the ice trade was reduced to between 20% and 50%" (Wikipedia, Ice trade — a whole-chain figure, harvest to customer). The *Tuscany*: 180 tons out; the Calcutta press *expected* "at least two-thirds" intact on arrival (≈120 tons), later accounts say ~100 tons landed (≈55%), and "the ratio of daily wastage increased rapidly whilst the ice was landing." Thoreau's crews "calculated that not twenty-five per cent of this would reach its destination, and that two or three per cent would be wasted in the cars." **Recommended on-air framing:** "In the early years most of what they cut never reached a customer. By the time of Calcutta, more than half a cargo could survive four months at sea and two equator crossings." Do not use a before/after percentage pair.

### The Gorrie foreshadow beat (~3 min; Cyrus structural, Jeff human)

- **Apalachicola.** A Gulf cotton port — "the third largest cotton" port on the Gulf when Gorrie arrived in 1833 (Becker 1935 via Google Books). Gorrie, a Nevis-born, New York–trained physician, became postmaster, councilman, bank director, and the town's fever doctor. (Sources: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Gorrie); [AOC](https://www.aoc.gov/explore-capitol-campus/art/john-gorrie-statue)).
- **Pans of ice over fever patients.** Working from miasma theory, he "suspended ice containers above patients' beds" and drew air over them into the sickroom, "reasoning that heat spread disease"; he believed "Nature would terminate the fevers by changing the seasons," so he would change the season indoors. (Sources: Mental Floss; AOC: "cooling sickrooms with ice to reduce fever.") The ice came from Boston — and when the ships didn't come, there was none. Hence the machine.
- **The machine (1844).** He "first mechanically produced ice in 1844," published articles under the pen name "Jenner" arguing for artificial cooling, and gave up his practice in 1845 to build it; by 1848 a working compressed-air machine "powered by horse, water, wind, or sail"; by 1850 "he could routinely manufacture ice bricks." (Sources: Wikipedia; Mental Floss.) *How it worked, one line for the writer (the mechanism itself is Wave 2's):* his own patent text says it — "the condensation of air by compression is accompanied by the development of heat, while… cold uniformly attends the expansion of air… The nature of my invention consists in taking advantage of this law to convert water into ice artificially by absorbing its heat of liquefaction with expanding air." (Source: [US Patent 8,080, Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8080A/en)). Compress air (it gets hot; dump the heat), let it expand (it gets cold), run it over brine to freeze water. An *air-cycle* machine — not the vapor-compression cycle that won.
- ⭐ **July 14, 1850 — Bastille Day at the Mansion House.** Apalachicola's summer ice shipment had not arrived. **Monsieur Rosan**, "a native of Paris and a resident cotton buyer" who was also French consul in the port (*History of Florida*, 1923; *Congressional Record* 1961), was giving a banquet at the Mansion House — "the largest hotel in Florida in ante-bellum days" — for Northern cotton brokers. When a guest wished aloud for ice for his wine, Rosan, who had seen the machine, "wagered a basket of champagne" that he could produce it; at the toast to the Republic, waiters carried in champagne buckets full of ice from Gorrie's machine. Gorrie's friend Dr. Alvan Chapman, the botanist, remembered Gorrie "excited and nervous." (Sources: Google Books snippets — *Ice and Cold Storage* 1913; *History of Florida* 1923; *Hygeia* 1939; *Congressional Record* 1961; *Florida's Past* 1996/2014; Shachtman 1999.) Date: 1850 is the documented date; 1847/48 circulate — use 1850.
- **The patent:** London patent 1850; **U.S. Patent No. 8,080, May 6, 1851**, "Improved process for the artificial production of ice," to "John Gorrie, of New Orleans, Louisiana." (Source: Google Patents.) Cyrus's "such a young patent number" line stands: eight thousand and eighty. Anchor: the U.S. has since issued more than eleven million (training data; `[VERIFY]` if used).
- **The backer.** Gorrie went to New Orleans to raise money for a full-scale plant (which is why the patent lists him there) and "finally sold a half interest to a man from Boston, whereby capital was pledged sufficient to carry out his purpose. The Boston partner, however, soon died, and the undertaking failed." (Source: *Ice and Refrigeration*, 1914.) The man's name "is not known" (*Florida Historical Quarterly*, 1947). `[GAP]` closed as unrecoverable.

- **Death.** June 29, 1855, Apalachicola, "suffering from a nervous collapse and devastated by failure" (Mental Floss); AOC: "Humiliated by criticism, financially ruined, and his health broken, Gorrie died in seclusion." Thirty-five years after he died broke, Southern ice factories were being named after him.
- ⭐ **The incumbent (Cyrus's structural read).** What Gorrie was up against in the 1850s, in period numbers: "Two million tons is a liberal estimate of the amount of ice stored… in 1855, in the United States, with six or seven million dollars of invested capital" (Hiles 1893). In 1847 alone, 353 ice-laden ships cleared Boston Harbor — about 75,000 tons exported and 27,000 used at home (Robichaud, BU). Fresh Pond in Cambridge had some fifty icehouses on its shores; Boston's harvest employed 1,200–1,500 men in season, and the whole country's ice trade "from 8,000 to 10,000" (*New-York Tribune*, 1858, via HRMM). *Today's fix sows the seeds of tomorrow's problems:* Tudor's solution built the capital — ponds, ships, icehouses, wharf rights, a workforce — that made the better solution un-fundable for a generation. Let it land through the Boston backer's death and the sneer, not as a slogan.

### Diffusion & Resistance

**Early adopters.** Caribbean planters and Havana cafés; Charleston and New Orleans saloons (the four picked in 1821); colonial Calcutta, Madras, and Bombay (where the Parsi firm of Jehangir Nusserwanji Wadia distributed the ice and Jamsetjee Jejeebhoy served the first ice creams at a dinner — Scroll.in; parked, not for air per Jeff's cut); hospitals ("some of the ice was reserved for medical hospitals in the presidency towns" — Scroll.in); London, where the Wenham Lake Ice Company opened at 125 Strand in summer 1844 with a block in the window and a newspaper behind it that passers-by could read *through* the ice, and won a royal warrant from Queen Victoria; Michael Faraday analyzed Wenham ice and found it so free of salts and air that it melted slower than ordinary ice; Florence Nightingale noted in 1859 that Wenham ice kept hospital milk from curdling. (Sources: [Wikipedia, Wenham Lake Ice Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wenham_Lake_Ice_Company); [LSE, Blain](https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/Research/GEHN/GEHNWP20-BB.pdf)). One line only.

**Resisters — and why they were reasonable:**
1. **The *Gazette* and Boston's shippers.** Ice melts; meltwater in a hold rots timbers and shifts ballast. Tudor had to buy his own brig (Investor Amnesia). True on the specifics.
2. **Mayor Roffignac.** Anchor 1. Public health in a fever city.
3. **Every northern town with a pond.** Why buy Boston ice when winter is free? Tudor's real market was where ice never formed; the North was a commodity fight he mostly didn't enter until the trade matured (training data framing; consistent with Hiles's "domestic and export trade were both of very slow growth" to 1825).
4. ⭐ **The natural-ice trade vs. Gorrie.** Above. The incumbent's arguments were also true on the specifics: the air-cycle machine was inefficient, and Gorrie's business plan required capital nobody had reason to risk when Boston ice was cheap most years. It was wrong on the trend — as Savannah proved in 1890.
5. **The Ice Trust** — the incumbent as villain rather than resister. Closing set piece, below.

**Enabling conditions.** Free insulation (sawdust, tan bark); Wyeth's plow and tools; ships that would otherwise sail south or east empty ("arranged the shipment as ballast for his empty vessels" — The Print); family capital; a colonial government willing to waive duties and let cargo land at night; the customers' own subscription icehouse; railroads for inland distribution (1830s on — training data); and, at the far end, the *habit*, deliberately built.

**Tipping point.** Calcutta, 1833: a stunt becomes a trade line with regular sailings from 1835. Then scale: by 1847, 353 ships a year out of Boston; by 1855, two million tons in storage.

### Scale — the Hudson Valley (1850s–1890s), with denominators

- **New York City's consumption:** 1854–55: 200,000 tons; 1855–56: 363,000; 1856–57 and 1857–58: 500,000 (*New-York Tribune*, Nov 1858, via HRMM). **1890: about 3 million tons a year** (Ancestry/Dalzell). Wikipedia (Ice trade): Hudson icehouses stored "around four million tons" in the 1880s. So the city's ice appetite grew roughly **fifteen-fold in thirty-five years** — a believable, anchored number.
- **Icehouses:** "135 large icehouses stored the product harvested between New York City and Albany. Most of them could hold between 20,000 and 85,000 tons of ice. Many more smaller ones that held between 3,000 and 5,000 tons also lined the banks." (Source: Rees, *Refrigeration Nation*, 2013, via Google Books.) Rockland Lake alone had houses of 40,000, 40,000, 30,000 and 20,000 tons; Athens had one holding 58,000 (*Tribune* 1858 via HRMM). Hiles (1893): "immense storage houses, where upward of one hundred thousand tons of ice are gathered under one roof."
- **Workers:** "employing 20,000 workers" on the Hudson in the 1880s (Wikipedia, Ice trade; Scenic Hudson gives the same figure — the site blocked fetching this pass); *Seaport* (1987): the New York companies "were employing upwards of 20,000 men. Some 3,000 horses and 1,500 wagons were bringing the ice to customers." Knickerbocker Ice alone: "dozens of steamboats, 75 ice barges, and… 3,000 workers" by the 1880s, with eight Manhattan depots by 1882. (Source: [Wikipedia, Knickerbocker Ice Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knickerbocker_Ice_Company)). `[FLAG: "~1,000 horses" on the Hudson harvest (overview) vs. "3,000 horses" on NYC delivery (Seaport) are different denominators — harvest vs. distribution. Say "thousands of horses" or specify.]` Lake Champlain's ~5,000 (overview) — not re-sourced this pass; `[VERIFY]` or drop.
- **Labor.** Winter work, "hard, back breaking, and cold wet work, the work day starting… at dawn and ending at dusk, six days a week," sawing, poling cakes down channels with pike poles, stacking in the houses; "the pay was often but $1.50 a day" (1850s), and men "strike for more money." (Source: HRMM, quoting *Tribune* 1858 and *Genesee Farmer* 1849.) Horses were shipped up-river on the steamboats each November (HRMM). **Who they were:** at Walden in 1847, Irish laborers under "Yankee overseers" (Thoreau); on the Hudson, farmers and river men idle in winter (HRMM implies; training data adds brickyard workers — `[VERIFY]`); in New York's delivery trade by the 1900s, largely southern Italian immigrants (Wikipedia, Iceman). `[MISSING PERSPECTIVE]` No first-person account from a Hudson ice cutter or Calcutta dock laborer was found; the script names who did the work where a source does (Thoreau) and does not ventriloquize. `[FLAG: the blueprint's "~$1/day" — the sourced 1850s figure is $1.50; say "a dollar or two a day."]`

### What Changed — the iceman's world (1880s–1890s)

- ⭐ **The window card.** A foot-square card printed with **25, 50, 75, 100** in the corners; you hung it in the window with the number of pounds you wanted upright, and the iceman, reading it from the street, cut the block to fit; if no card showed, he drove by. Different companies' icemen carried different-colored cards. (Sources: Google Books snippets — *Home Sweet Home in the Nineteenth Century* 1965; *Vanishing Americana* 1961; *The Indiana Years* 1983; several memoirs.) Arthur Miller remembered icemen in "leather vests and a wet piece of sackcloth slung over the right shoulder" who, after sliding the block into the box, "invariably slipped the sacking off and stood there waiting, dripping, for their money." Icemen "began their day at 4:00 am." (Source: [Wikipedia, Iceman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceman_(occupation))). `[FLAG: "sheepskin" is the blueprint's word; sources say leather/sackcloth. Use "a wet sack on the shoulder."]`
- **The icebox.** A wooden cabinet with hollow walls "lined with tin or zinc and packed with… cork, sawdust, straw, or seaweed"; the block sat in a top compartment and cold air sank over the food below; better boxes had a spigot for the catch pan, cheaper ones "a drip pan… placed under the box [that] has to be emptied at least daily" — or it floods the kitchen floor. (Source: [Wikipedia, Icebox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icebox)). `[VERIFY]` the blueprint's "by the 1890s 'all but the poorest' had an icebox (Smithsonian NMAH)" — Smithsonian collections pages were blocked this pass; Hiles (1893) says only that "the users of ice, directly or indirectly, now include nearly the entire population."
- **New commodities:** ice cream and iced drinks (Hiles: "the demand for ice creams and cooled drinks, together with the growing taste for luxuries"); lager — "the brewers, who in earlier days had suspended operations during the heat of the summer, now pursued their avocation continuously, with the aid of ice" (Hiles) — the American version of Bavaria's 1553 rule; meat packers and fisheries; and Civil War hospitals ("During the war of the Rebellion, the Government was a large purchaser, on account of the hospital service" — Hiles). Fresh fish "frozen in the center of cakes of ice" for shipment (Hiles).
- **A global commodity in frozen water.** Norway supplied more than 99% of Britain's natural ice around 1900 — about half a million tons a year (1899: 504,627 tons to Britain; everyone else combined, 515 tons) — and a Norwegian lake, Oppegård, was renamed "Wenham Lake" so its ice could be sold in London under the famous American name. Carlo Gatti's first Norwegian consignment reached London in 1857 (400 tons). (Sources: [LSE, Blain](https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/Research/GEHN/GEHNWP20-BB.pdf); [Canal Museum](https://www.canalmuseum.org.uk/ice/iceimport.htm); Wikipedia, Wenham.)
- ⭐ **Frozen aside (confirmed).** Disney's *Frozen* (2013) opens on "Frozen Heart" — ice harvesters on a frozen lake sawing blocks and loading a huge sled, the saws' rhythm driving the song, young Kristoff and Sven trying to keep up; Robert Lopez modeled it on "Fathoms Below." (Source: [Wikipedia, Frozen Heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frozen_Heart)). Place at the harvest.

### Closing set piece — the Ice Trust and the nickel piece (New York, 1900)

- **The company.** Morse organized Consolidated Ice in 1897; in 1899 it merged with Knickerbocker and others into the **American Ice Company**, "grossly overcapitalized at **$60 million**." (Source: Wikipedia, Morse.) A Minnesota paper's OCR of the May 1900 inquiry: "capital of $60,000,000." It controlled the Hudson and Maine (Kennebec/Penobscot) supply *and* the piers: "There are only one or two ice bridges (piers) left at which independent dealers can unload their ice… All the big bridges are in control of the [trust]," an Albany independent told the *Tribune*; "He can ruin any independent dealer in this town and we all know it." (Source: [New-York Tribune, May 9, 1900, p. 2](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030214/1900-05-09/ed-1/?sp=2)). Wikipedia (Van Wyck): "the sole company permitted to land ice at New York piers."
- ⭐ **The price.** "On **May 1** [1900] the American Ice Company notified its customers that the price of ice for the season would be **60 cents for 100 pounds**, and that **no small pieces would be sold**." (Source: *Appleton's Annual Cyclopaedia* for 1900, via Google Books.) Against "25 cents last year" (*Commercial & Financial Chronicle*, 1900) or 30 cents (other period sources). The trust's stated reason: the ice crop had been short. The *Literary Digest* did the arithmetic: the trust's ice cost it "less than $1.50 a ton" and 60¢ a hundred is "$12 a ton." (Google Books snippet, 1900.)
- **The nickel piece.** Tenement families bought ice in five- and ten-cent pieces, daily, because they had no place to keep a block and no money to buy one; the piece kept the baby's milk from turning in a room with no cross-breeze (LESHP; Jeff should carry the swill-milk callback from the Opening). Abolishing the small pieces was the price increase *for the poor*. Within two weeks, under a press storm, the company began "to sell five-cent lumps of ice to the poor of the East Side" from twenty wagons — but at the depots only "to those who applied… with **certificates from any charitable organization**," which the *Tribune* called "a miserable subterfuge… almost fiendish in the forfeiture of self-respect which it inflicts": "Why… should any decent man in this proud city be forced to send his wife or child out into the street to vouch for his poverty?" Those who could afford it bought from independent peddlers instead. (Source: [New-York Tribune, May 17, 1900, p. 3](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030214/1900-05-17/ed-1/?sp=3)). A cool wave broke the first heat: "ICE FAMINE IS OVER — Cool Wave Gives Relief" (*Evening World*, May 16). So: the nickel piece came back within weeks — with a humiliation attached.
- ⭐ **Cyrus's surge-pricing beat — the market-structure facts.** By late June the *Tribune* found the trust "selling ice in this city at as many prices as the fakirs in Fulton Street. To the rich the trust sells ice at **65 cents**; to the poor in the tenement houses the price is as low as **33 cents**, and to the rest of the people prices range all the way between… One ice cart of the American Ice Company in Harlem yesterday sold at **four different prices along the length of a single block**… the laws of supply and demand which govern other lines of trade appear to have no influence. The reason is soon found when it is understood that **the trust is practically the supply**." And: the trust bid to supply the federal buildings (Post Office, Custom House, Appraisers' Stores, Naval Station, Barge Office) at **25 cents a hundred** — "In other words, the trust can sell ice to certain customers as low as 25 cents and make money." (Source: [New-York Tribune, June 29, 1900, p. 9](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030214/1900-06-29/ed-1/?sp=9)). Independents were meanwhile selling below the trust (LESHP; *Tribune* May 17). That is not surge pricing — it is price discrimination by a monopolist who owns the piers; Cyrus can say both.
- **The press.** Hearst's *New York Journal* led the attack on Tammany ownership (LESHP; Wikipedia, Morse: the *Journal and Advertiser* exposed the arrangements); Pulitzer's *World* went to court — it obtained the summons that put the mayor on the stand and hired **Wheeler H. Peckham**, "the counsel who prosecuted Tweed," to cross-examine (*Evening World*, May 26 and June 9, 1900). Both Hearst and Pulitzer papers; the *Tribune* and *Times* piled on. Jeff should not credit Hearst alone.
- ⭐ **The mayor on the stand.** May 26, 1900: "Mayor Van Wyck, John F. Carroll [Croker's deputy], Dock Commissioners Murphy, Cram and Meyer, President Morse and the other ice barons appeared before **Justice Gaynor in the Supreme Court in Brooklyn**… making inquiry into the formation of the gigantic Ice Trust" — the courtroom crowded, Morse's counsel De Lancey Nicoll pleading for delay. (Source: [Evening World, May 26, 1900, p. 2](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030193/1900-05-26/ed-1/?sp=2); [Daily Kennebec Journal, June 1, 1900](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn82014248/1900-06-01/ed-1/?sp=6)). The dock commissioners were in the room because the *piers* were the monopoly. **June 9, 1900**, the *Evening World*'s front page: "VAN WYCK FLATLY CONTRADICTED — Swears He Owns 4,200 Shares of Ice Stock — Swears He Owes $150,000 for Them — Swears He Borrowed $200,000 from the Garfield National Bank on the Stock — The Garfield National Bank President Swears He Never Loaned the Mayor a Cent." (Source: [Evening World, June 9, 1900, p. 1](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030193/1900-06-09/ed-1/?sp=1)). The *Tribune* headlined the testimony "THE MAYOR'S SORRY SHOW" and quoted him claiming he'd "heard a little about" the agitation against the trust. (Source: [New-York Tribune, June 10, 1900](https://www.loc.gov/resource/sn83030214/1900-06-10/ed-1/?sp=6)). His brother Augustus, Carroll, and Croker's family held stock; Croker's ran to six figures in his wife's name (Brownstoner). Later reporting broke the mayor's holding into 2,660 preferred and 3,325 common (LESHP); at par about $680,000 — on a salary of $15,000 (Wikipedia). Morse had reportedly *lent* him the money (Bowdoin Daily Sun via overview; the sworn testimony says a bank did, and the bank said no).
- **Croker's letter.** June 15, 1900, *Evening World* front page: "THE BOSS TALKS ON ICE — 'If it were in my power or in the power of Tammany Hall to give the poor ice for nothing we would do so' — Richard Croker" — printed above a paragraph noting how many shares of preferred and common Croker, his son Frank, and Mrs. Elizabeth Croker held. - **Prices afterward.** The trust cut prices under pressure but they stayed "a third higher than… at the start" of the season (Brownstoner). The five-cent piece was back by mid-May.
- **The election.** Van Wyck did not run again; in **November 1901** Seth Low, Columbia's president, on a Republican-Fusion ticket, beat Tammany's Edward Shepard **296,813 to 265,177**. - **Why it matters / the bridge.** A city that had learned to *expect* cold discovered in one summer that its cold was controlled by weather (the short crop) and by Tammany (the piers). Both problems have the same answer, and it was already running in Savannah.

### Consequences & Changes — winners and losers

**Winners:** Tudor and Boston merchant capital (Tudor ~$200M today at death); Wyeth (a fortune after Oregon); the Knickerbocker and Hudson ice companies; bartenders, confectioners, brewers, packers; colonial elites; Morse. **Losers:** Gorrie and his nameless backer; the tenement poor, who paid the most per pound and were made to prove poverty for a nickel's ice; the independents crushed at the piers; and — planted — the twenty thousand winter jobs on the Hudson and the icemen, whose trade the next two waves end. Hiles (1893), the trade's own voice: "not many years after this, ice and refrigerator machines began to supply the demand for ice in tropical climes, and the importations of the natural product soon ceased" — the export trade was *already* dead of machines by the 1890s, and the industry knew it.

### Road Not Taken

- **Gorrie finds capital in 1851.** If the Boston partner lives and a full-scale air-cycle plant goes up in New Orleans in the early 1850s, machine ice arrives in the North's cities in the 1860s–70s rather than the 1900s. Concretely: the Hudson never grows to 135 big icehouses and 20,000 winter jobs; there is no pier monopoly for Morse to buy; New York's 1900 summer is uneventful; and refrigeration diffuses *from medicine outward* — from fever wards, which is where Gorrie started — rather than from cocktails and claret. The catch, honestly: Gorrie's air cycle was inefficient; it was Carré's ammonia (1859) and Linde's compressor (1876) that made machine ice cheap. The counterfactual is "a decade or two sooner," not "instantly." (Sources: patent text; Wave 2 material in overview.)
- **Britain stays on Norwegian ice into the 1910s.** Norway had "comparative advantage, proximity to Britain and long-standing trade relations," and British consumers were reluctant to use artificial ice; so Britain — the world's most industrial economy — ran on cut lake ice from Norway (>99% of imports c. 1900; ~half a million tons a year) until World War I, when "within a decade, the market for natural ice more or less disappeared. Norwegian exports of ice in 1920 were only 5 per cent of the levels of 1910." (Source: [LSE, Blain, GEHN WP 20/06](https://www.lse.ac.uk/Economic-History/Assets/Documents/Research/GEHN/GEHNWP20-BB.pdf)). The other world: natural ice was not doomed by physics — where it was cheap and near, it lasted until a war broke the shipping. America's natural-ice trade died faster because America had a *South* with no ice and a war that cut it off.

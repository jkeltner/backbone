# Chapter 04 Research: WAVE 3 — Cold Comes Home (1907–1950)

## Chapter Context

**Blueprint summary (lines 291–368):** A chemist who had to hide her sex to get hired writes the rules that make stored food trustworthy; an industry throws a banquet of months-old food to prove nobody dies. The machine shrinks toward the kitchen — belt-driven, leaky, lethal (Chicago, August 1929). Einstein and Szilárd try to build a refrigerator with no moving parts and lose to a General Motors chemist who finds a gas that can't burn and can't poison you, and blows out a candle with it. GE welds the compressor shut; the utilities discover a refrigerator roughly doubles a household's bill forever and finance it on the light bill; the machine-ice industry — last wave's winner — fights the electric box; and the refrigerator goes from under one in ten American homes to nearly half in the ten worst years of the American economy. The chapter ends on the triumph of the safe gas, which is the next chapter's problem.

## Deep Research

### Narrative Arc

1. **1907 — Trust before machines.** Cold storage works; the public doesn't believe in it. The USDA's Bureau of Chemistry hires a Philadelphia chemist under her initials to write the rules. She puts thermometers in railcars and tells the country the truth: a bad egg in May "is as rare a phenomenon as snow in the tropics."
2. **1911 — Theater and statutes.** The industry feeds Chicago's mayor months-old food; Massachusetts and New York write date-stamps and time limits into law.
3. **1918–1929 — The machine comes indoors and kills people.** Kelvinator (1918), Frigidaire (GM, 1919), belt-driven compressors and toxic gases. August 1929: Chicago's Painter family. The American Medical Association counts twenty-nine poisonings and ten deaths in Chicago that year. Same spring, the Cleveland Clinic fire.
4. **1926–1932 — Einstein reads the newspaper.** Two theoretical physicists try to design the leak out of existence; AEG builds it; it howls; it works; the Depression, Freon, and Hitler shelve it.
5. **1927–1930 — The seal and the safe gas.** Steenstrup welds the compressor shut; Midgley finds fluorine on the periodic table and blows out a candle with the result.
6. **1930–1940 — The utilities sell the box on the light bill.** Thirty-five months to pay, charges on the electric bill; the TVA sells an eighty-dollar refrigerator in Tupelo; the machine-ice industry — at its all-time peak — fights back with "ice is clean, dry, and cold."
7. **1940 — The census.** Forty-four percent mechanical, twenty-seven percent ice, twenty-seven percent nothing. By 1950, four in five. The iceman is gone. The gas inside every box is Freon.

### Anchor Stories (Fully Developed)

#### Anchor 1 — "M.E. Pennington" (Philadelphia and Washington, 1907) — Jeff leads

- **Who:** Mary Engle Pennington, 34, Ph.D., proprietor of the Philadelphia Clinical Laboratory; Harvey W. Wiley, chief of the USDA Bureau of Chemistry.
- **When:** 1907 (exam and hiring); chief of the new Food Research Laboratory by 1908.
- **Where:** Philadelphia; the Bureau of Chemistry, Washington.
- **Setup:** Wiley had just won the Pure Food and Drugs Act (1906). The next fight was perishables — eggs, poultry, milk, fish — and the new cold-storage warehouses the public didn't trust. He had been working with Pennington on cold-storage problems since 1905 (FDA) and wanted her to run a new lab. Federal hiring went through a Civil Service examination and board.
- **What happened (verified, three institutional accounts agree):** She sat the exam and "achieved the top score" (FDA). Wiley "disguised the fact that she was female by submitting his hiring request under the name of M.E. Pennington" (FDA); NIHF: he "altered her name to 'M.E. Pennington' to conceal her gender during selection." "When his ruse was discovered, he prevailed by arguing that the civil service law did not allow him to not hire her just because she was a woman" (FDA). SHI adds that the Civil Service "attempted rescission upon discovering her sex; Wiley intervened." She became the Bureau's first female lab chief and "the highest-paid federal woman at the time" (SHI). `[FLAG]` Blueprint says she took the exam "at Wiley's suggestion" as M.E. — the sources say *Wiley* did the filing; she took the exam under her own name and scored highest. Script should give Wiley the maneuver and her the score.

#### The toxic-gas problem as a systems failure (Cyrus leads)

- **What was in the box:** the three refrigerants in common household use in the 1920s were **methyl chloride, ammonia, and sulfur dioxide** — "all toxic, and the quantities in a refrigerator could kill" (Dannen). They were chosen for physics — a boiling point and working pressure a small compressor could handle — not for what happened when they got out. Sulfur dioxide is "corrosive to the eyes" (Wikipedia, *Refrigerator*); Frigidaire's early units used SO₂ — training data, `[VERIFY]`; methyl chloride was, in the AMA's words, "one of the most popular refrigerants used in mechanical refrigeration" and is nearly odorless. Chicago's toxicologist, Dr. William D. McNally, told the *Tribune* the gas "makes their noses run" and causes "eyes to burn" ([Dead in Chicago, Part 2](https://www.deadinchicago.com/post/179217013289/19-october-killed-by-the-fridge-part-2)).
- **Why the leaks happened (Dannen, paraphrasing Einstein and Szilárd's own reasoning):** "the problem was not just the refrigerant. Such leakages, from bearings and seals, were inevitable in systems with moving parts." Belt-driven compressors on top of the box; a rotating shaft that had to pass through a wall; a seal that wore.
- ⭐ **The Chicago cluster, 1929 (verified):** the American Medical Association's committee reported **"twenty-nine instances" of methyl-chloride poisoning in Chicago in 1929, of which "ten patients… died"** (cited in Albert Weinstein, "Methyl Chloride (Refrigerator) Gas Poisoning," *JAMA* 108:19, May 8, 1937, which references the AMA's 1929 committee report and its control recommendations). The family of three is the **Painter family** — two parents and a one-year-old — killed by a heavy leak from the apartment building's central refrigeration unit after the maintenance man heard a hiss, couldn't find the leak, "and left it for a night, the night in which the Painter family met their end." "Immediately, city officials called for a ban on the gas, which companies seemed to be in favor for, though they preferred to have it as an option until a better refrigerant could be found" (Dead in Chicago, Part 1, from *Tribune* coverage). A precursor cluster: August 1928, 1940 North Lincoln Avenue — newlyweds Joseph and Catherine Magliano, both in their mid-twenties, and a sixth-floor nurse, Alma Lee, all dead (*Chicago Tribune*, Aug 12–13, 1928, via Dead in Chicago). Chicago later required odorants in refrigerant gas. `[GAP]` The Painters' first names, address and exact August date were not in the reachable sources; the *Tribune* archive was not accessible. `[FLAG]` The blueprint's "rash of deaths" phrasing is fine; the countable number is the AMA's: twenty-nine poisoned, ten dead, one city, one year.
- **Cleveland Clinic, May 15, 1929 (short, factual, corrected):** At about 11:30 a.m. in the clinic's basement, three to four tons of **nitrocellulose X-ray film** began to decompose and burn — Case Western's encyclopedia attributes ignition to "an unguarded 100-watt incandescent light bulb"; Cleveland Historical to a steamfitter's repair of a leaking steam pipe (the two accounts differ; say "heat from a bulb or a steam pipe"). Victims died of nitrogen oxides ("nitrogen peroxide") and, by some accounts, phosgene. Policeman Ernest Staab carried out 21 people and never recovered his health. - **The Berlin family (Einstein's reported motive):** Dannen, citing MIT physicist Bernard Feld, "who heard the story from Szilard": "One day Einstein read about an entire family — parents and several children — who had been killed in their beds by the poisonous gases leaking from the pump of their refrigerator." The date is "evidently the winter of 1925–1926." No name, newspaper, or address has ever been identified. Per Jeff: "reportedly" is fine.

#### Anchor 2 — Einstein reads the newspaper (Berlin, 1926) — Cyrus leads

- **Who:** Albert Einstein; Leó Szilárd; engineer Albert Korodi; the AEG Research Institute, Berlin.
- **When:** winter 1925–26 (the newspaper story); first patent filings early 1926; AEG contract fall 1928; a working refrigerator in continuous operation July 31, 1931; final report August 16, 1932.
- **Where:** Einstein's Berlin apartment; the Institute of Technology in Berlin (first prototypes); AEG's research institute.
- **Setup:** By the mid-1920s Szilárd was "a frequent visitor to Einstein's home." He was about to become a *privatdozent* — a lecturer paid only by student fees. "The inventions, if successful, could support Szilard's budding career" (Dannen).
- **What happened, beat by beat (all Dannen unless noted):**
  1. The newspaper. "Einstein was distressed by the tragedy. **'There must be a better way,'** he said to Szilard." (Feld's recollection of Szilárd's account — the reported quote; the blueprint's "there must be a better design" should become "a better way.")
  2. The reasoning. The problem was not only the gas; it was the moving parts. "From their knowledge of thermodynamics… they could derive many ways to produce cooling without mechanical motion. Why not put these to use?"
  3. The deal. A letter from Szilárd to Einstein preserves the terms: all refrigeration inventions joint property; Szilárd gets first claim on profits if his income falls below a university assistant's salary; otherwise royalties shared equally.
  4. Three designs, not one. By fall 1926 they had settled on three principles — **absorption, diffusion, electromagnetism** — and Szilárd wrote his brother Béla (October 1926): "All three machines work without moving parts, and are hermetically sealed…. One of these three types is nearly identical with one of the Electrolux company's machines (in my opinion the best at the moment)…. The other two types are completely different from any other machines known until now." Einstein's patent-examiner years meant they drafted the filings themselves. Korodi on Einstein's contribution: he "proposed a quite simple and cheap system especially suited for small refrigerators" — his answer to "the diabolical complexity of absorption designs."
  5. The American patent attorney's reply to the U.S. filing: "I would be interested to know if Albert Einstein is the same person who propounded the theory of relativity." (U.S. Patent 1,781,541, filed Dec 16, 1927, granted **Nov 11, 1930** — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_refrigerator).)
  6. Electrolux buys. December 2, 1927: Platen-Munters Refrigerating System, a division of AB Electrolux in Stockholm, bought the absorption-refrigerator application for **3,150 reichsmarks — $750** — a price Electrolux's files call "very cheap." (About $10,000 in 1997 dollars.) Electrolux later bought the diffusion design too, and "never develop[ed] either… the firm bought the designs mostly to safeguard their own pending applications."
  7. The Citogel cooler (Hamburg, 1928). A small immersion cooler "that could be dipped for instance in a cup of some beverage," run off nothing but tap-water pressure — Einstein's idea to use methanol as a cheap, replaceable working fluid ("That was the idea of Einstein," Korodi). Demonstrated at the Leipzig Fair, early 1928. Killed by the German water system: tap pressure "varied between buildings as well as from floor to floor within a building."
  8. ⭐ The electromagnetic pump. A pump with **no mechanical moving parts**: a traveling electromagnetic field drove a liquid metal, which acted as the piston compressing a refrigerant. Mercury was the obvious fluid but conducted poorly; Szilárd proposed a **potassium-sodium alloy**, liquid above −11°C. It attacked the wire insulation. "Szilard took the problem back to Einstein. 'Einstein thought a few minutes,' Korodi related, and proposed… induction" — drive the metal from outside the tube. Korodi calculated it would be "still much less efficient than standard compressors. What it lacked in efficiency, however, it gained in reliability. 'It was worthwhile to make such a compressor,' he stated: an Einstein-Szilard pump would not leak or fail."
  9. AEG (fall 1928) set up a special department with two full-time engineers; Szilárd was "consultant." Salaries: 500 reichsmarks a month (~$120) for Korodi — "a good salary… at a time when a car, a Ford, cost $300"; for Szilárd, royalties and fees brought "a comfortable $3,000 a year (worth roughly $40,000 today)." Korodi: "I didn't talk to Einstein about physics."
  10. ⭐ The noise. Cavitation in the liquid metal. Dennis Gabor: it "howled like a jackal." Philip Morrison: it wailed "like a banshee." Korodi: like rushing water. Voltage tricks at the start of each stroke "eventually lowered the noise to acceptable levels."
  11. It worked. "In two years," Korodi said, "a complete refrigerator was built, which worked — operated — as a refrigerator." **On July 31, 1931**, an Einstein–Szilárd refrigerator "went into continuous operation at the A.E.G. Research Institute," mounted in the cabinet of a four-cubic-foot **General Electric model G40** — with a potassium-sodium pump and pentane refrigerant, drawing **136 watts, 2.3 kilowatt-hours a day**. A four-month run on pure potassium raised electrical efficiency from 16 to 26 percent.
  12. The politics. September 14, 1930: the Nazis take nearly 20% in the Reichstag election. Szilárd to Einstein, September 27: "From week to week I detect new symptoms, if my nose doesn't deceive me, that peaceful [political] development in Europe in the next ten years is not to be counted on…. Indeed, I don't know if it will be possible to build our refrigerator in Europe."
  13. The end. "But for the A.E.G., battered by the growing worldwide depression, the refrigerator was not good enough. Improvements in conventional refrigerators… were shrinking the potential market. **The 1930 American demonstration of a nontoxic 'Freon' refrigerant, in particular, promised to eliminate the danger of leaks.**" In 1932 the AEG institute was cut in half; Korodi wrote the 104-page final report, *A.E.G. Technischer Bericht 689*, August 16, 1932 — his copy is the only one; AEG's files burned in the war. "Only months later Adolf Hitler's appointment as chancellor ended Berlin's golden age of physics."
- **Aftermath:** "In the seven years of their collaboration, Szilard and Einstein filed **more than 45 patent applications in at least six countries.**" The pump "eventually proved its value. The built-in safety of its design later found a more critical task in cooling breeder reactors." Szilárd's patent savings carried him through two years in Britain — "Szilard's early research on atomic energy was in fact made possible by this money"; he conceived the neutron chain reaction in autumn 1933. **July 1939:** Szilárd visited Einstein on Long Island and "convinced Einstein to write his famous letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt" — and, Dannen notes, that same day the two were reminiscing about the refrigerators.
- **Cyrus's structural read (grounded):** Absorption didn't lose on merit; it lost on *two* fronts at once. (1) The grid made a motor cheap — a compressor is a small electric motor, and by 1929 68% of American homes had a wire (Paleofuture). (2) Freon erased absorption's one selling point. Dannen says it in one sentence: the Freon demonstration "promised to eliminate the danger of leaks." And the gas-absorption path stayed alive anyway — **Servel** bought the Swedish (Platen–Munters/Electrolux) rights in 1925, sold its first gas refrigerator in 1926, and was "the only American manufacturer of gas refrigerators" from 1927 until it stopped in **1956** ([Wikipedia, Servel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servel)). It was a real product for thirty years. It just never got to eighty percent.
- ⭐ **"The better answer has real costs too" (Jeff's read, now with numbers):** Korodi's own calculation was that the electromagnetic pump was "much less efficient than standard compressors"; the 1931 prototype drew 2.3 kWh a day — roughly twice what a compressor box of its size drew (`[VERIFY]` the compressor figure; see break-even below). Gas absorption needed a gas line and a flame in the kitchen. And it carried its own hazard: in 1998 the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission was still warning owners of old Servel gas refrigerators and paying them to scrap the units — carbon monoxide (Wikipedia, *Refrigerator*, CPSC 1998 — `[VERIFY]` the CO detail; the CPSC warning is confirmed). Nobody chose wrong in 1930. The script should not lean this toward climate.

- **Sources:** [Dannen, "The Einstein–Szilard Refrigerators," *Scientific American*, Jan 1997 (SMU PDF)](https://www.physics.smu.edu/scalise/P3374fa16/EinsteinSzilardRefrigerators.pdf) — the definitive account, read in full; [Wikipedia, Einstein refrigerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_refrigerator); [Wikipedia, Servel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Servel).

#### Closing scene — Midgley blows out the candle (1928–1930) — Cyrus leads

- **The brief:** Frigidaire (GM) needed a refrigerant "particularly… for use in air conditioning which would not take fire and which would be free of harmful effects upon people who might be exposed to it" (Kettering memoir). Kettering: "It took a whole Saturday afternoon to sell Midge on the idea that this was quite an important project" (Invention & Technology). **Fall 1928**, Ohio State University laboratories, Columbus, with **Albert Henne** (and, per Wikipedia's *Dichlorodifluoromethane* entry, **Robert McNary**).
- ⭐ **The method (Kettering's account — the primary):** "Again with the periodic table as a guide, Midgley came to the conclusion that any *new* compound which could have suitable physical properties, would have to contain fluorine. And so, in spite of warnings about the hazardous nature of fluorine and of such misgivings as he himself may have had, he and his helpers prepared such a compound, dichlorodifluoromethane." Midgley's own framing, from his 1937 Perkin Medal address "From the Periodic Table to Production": the table was the guide to the "fox hunt" that found tetraethyl lead and it "pointed the way for him to make use of the unpromising but effective element, fluorine." Invention & Technology gives the reasoning as he told it: flammability fell moving right across the table, toxicity fell moving up — the arrows crossed at fluorine.
- ⭐ **The near miss (Kettering):** "But he almost missed that important discovery; for, of the three or four small batches of the new compound made with the total available raw material of the time, only the first was not toxic to the animals on which it was tested. The others behaved just as everyone expected fluorine compounds to behave, that is, the animals exposed to their vapor quickly died. But that, as it was found later, was due not to the Freon itself but to an unsuspected impurity in the compound. Fortunately, though, it was the first batch which turned out well." (The impure antimony-trifluoride bottles are the mechanism usually given — training data.) The final animal test replaced "all the nitrogen in the air the animals breathed… with Freon vapor without harm to them."
- **"Three days":** repeated by Smithsonian, CNN and most secondary accounts; it is *not* in Kettering's memoir. `[VERIFY]` against the Perkin address before saying "three days" flatly; "within days" is safe.
- ⭐ **The candle (Kettering — verified):** "in presenting the first account of the research which yielded the fluorine-containing refrigerants, he demonstrated both their nontoxic and their noninflammable properties in one breath, so to speak. This he did by taking his lungs full of the vapor of one of the compounds and then softly exhaling it to surround and extinguish a candle burning before him." The "first account" is Midgley & Henne, "Organic Fluorides as Refrigerants," *Industrial & Engineering Chemistry* 22:542 (1930) (Semantic Scholar confirms title/authors/year); it was presented at the American Chemical Society's meeting in **Atlanta, April 1930** — training data, `[VERIFY]` the city and month.
- **The verdict, in Kettering's words:** fluorine yielded "an altogether new series of refrigerating compounds, the only such compounds known which are stable, non-inflammable, and completely nontoxic, and which are therefore indispensable in air-conditioning." ⭐ *That sentence is the triumph the chapter ends on — and every word of it is the next chapter's problem.*

### Resistance & Diffusion

#### Resistance 1 — The machine-ice industry vs. the electric refrigerator (Jeff's winner-becomes-resister beat)

- ⭐ **Its real size at its peak (1920 Census of Manufactures, 1919 data — verified from the volume):** 2,867 plants; 38,605 people engaged (30,247 wage earners on average — 41,078 in July, 20,796 in January); 27.9 million tons of ice a year; $137 million of product. Twenty years earlier (1899): 775 plants and 4.3 million tons. **Manufactured ice grew six-and-a-half-fold in two decades** — it had beaten the ponds (a design historian's figure: by 1914 the U.S. made 26 million tons of artificial ice against 24 million harvested — [JSTOR Daily, citing Isenstadt](https://daily.jstor.org/on-the-rocks/), `[VERIFY]`). And it was at that peak — 1919 — that Kelvinator and Frigidaire were shipping their first thousands. Do not use the overview's 4,800 / 160,000 / 40 million; the census does not support them. (The delivery trade — icemen, wagons — is not in the manufacturing census; "tens of thousands of icemen" is safe, a specific number is not.)
- **Its arguments — and why they were reasonable:** (1) Danger: methyl chloride and sulfur dioxide *did* kill people — twenty-nine poisoned, ten dead in Chicago in 1929 (AMA). True until Freon. (2) Cost: $600 in 1920, $268 by 1929 — true. (3) Reliability: ComEd installed 279 boxes in nearly two years in 1923–24 (Tobey) — true early. (4) Quality: ice circulated moist air; "Ice keeps food better than any other refrigerant. Ice is clean, dry, and cold. It preserves the full flavour and goodness of all foods. And ice is so economical!" — an "I'm For Ice" ad quoted in the only source found ([blog](https://rochemamabolo.wordpress.com/2023/11/11/enemies-of-innovation-chilling-wars-between-iceboxes-and-refrigerators/), uncited — `[VERIFY]` before quoting). The verified version of the "cold alone is not enough" argument is Pennington's own 1927 pamphlet title for the ice trade, *Cold Is the Absence of Heat*, and the fact that the National Association of Ice Industries put the government's cold-chain authority on its payroll to write consumer pamphlets (Wikipedia, `[VERIFY]` against Rees). ⭐ *That* is the winner-becomes-resister beat with a name on it: the woman who wrote the rules for the cold chain spent the 1920s writing pamphlets for the ice industry.
- **How the fight actually looked on the ground (period trade press):** Chicago's Wieboldt's department store sold refrigerators for three years on "**meter ice**" — a coin meter on the box, the customer feeding it the way she'd paid the iceman; ComEd's Reace in 1935: thirty-five months on the electric bill "is better even than the meter ice proposition" (*Electrical Merchandising*, March 1935). The 1935 salesman's worksheet begins with the line the fridge replaces: "**Ice formerly purchased** ($ per year) … ¢ per day," then "Food spoilage prevented," then "Saving through buying food in larger quantities (fruits, vegetables, meats, canned goods — at least a week's supply instead of one day or one meal at a time)," less "operating costs," equals "NET SAVINGS, per month" versus "necessary monthly payments." ⭐ The iceman was literally the first line crossed out.
- **When it ended:** In 1940, 27% of American homes still ran on ice (census). By 1950 about 80% had mechanical refrigeration (overview; 1950 census — `[VERIFY]` exact). Big-city home ice delivery collapsed across the 1940s–50s; no single documented end date was found — say "within about a decade of the war." Ice harvesting itself "persisted until the 1950s" (JSTOR Daily).
- **The honest cost (Jeff, plainly, once):** the FEE essay the hosts endorsed makes the case and concedes the loss in the same breath: "icemen lost" and "received a smaller piece from a larger economic pie" ([Van Cott, FEE](https://fee.org/articles/the-icemans-job-was-destroyed-good/)). A real person with a route, a horse, and customers who knew his name — and the utility that sold the box profited on both ends of him. Both true.

#### Resistance 2 — Cost and readiness

- **ComEd, 1923–24 (verified):** Commonwealth Edison "contracted with a manufacturer for a single small standard box to sell to customers. In 1923 and eleven months of 1924, the giant utility installed only **279** refrigerators" (Tobey, ch. 1). Twelve years later the same utility's refrigeration sales supervisor reported "approximate 30 per cent saturation" in Chicago (March 1935).
- **Price and the Depression:** $600 (1920) → $268 average advertised (1929, Tobey) / $275 (1930, AEI) → $152 (1940, AEI). The TVA-emblem refrigerator sold for **$80** in 1934. Consumer prices fell about a quarter from 1929 to 1933 (training data) — the box fell faster than wages.
- **The trade's own worry (June 1934 editorial, *Electrical Merchandising*):** "we get an uncomfortable eyeful of low down payment and long term offers. If pressure of competition in terms becomes any greater, the housewife will begin to regard ten-cents-a-day for refrigeration as a gross extravagance. This type of selling has gone far enough." Washington and Baltimore ads the same month: Frigidaire from $114.50, "no down payment and 15 to 30 cents a day"; a Reno dealer: "your old range as down payment and home wired free, at $6.50 per month."

#### The utility break-even (Cyrus's razor-and-blades — the math, done)

- **Base load (Federal Power Commission, 1935, as cited by Kitchens & Jaworski, NBER WP 22254):** "In 1935, between **15 and 40 kWh per month** was enough electricity for lighting and smaller appliances, up to 150 kWh was enough to add refrigeration, 250 kWh included cooking, and 500 kWh allowed for the hot water heating." Georgia Power's *average* domestic customer used **903.7 kWh a year — about 75 a month** — in early 1935, and the company was "shooting for" 1,000 (*Electrical Merchandising*, March 1935).
- **Price (verified example, 1935):** Edison Electric Illuminating in Acton, Mass., charged $1.05 for 15 kWh (**7.0¢/kWh**) and $1.65 for 25 kWh (**6.6¢**) — a declining-block rate, so the *next* kilowatt-hours were cheaper than the first ([NBER WP 22254](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22254/w22254.pdf)). National average residential revenue per kWh was roughly 6¢ in 1930 and under 4¢ by 1940 — training data, `[VERIFY]` (Historical Statistics of the U.S.).
- **Fridge draw:** Dannen's figure for the Einstein–Szilárd prototype — 136 W, **2.3 kWh a day** — is the only primary number found this pass; a 1930s compressor box drew roughly **1 to 1.5 kWh a day** (30–45 kWh/month) (ACHR via overview, 20–50 — `[VERIFY]`).
- ⭐ **The arithmetic, spelled out for the script:** A lights-and-radio household at 25 kWh a month pays about $1.65. Add a refrigerator at 30–45 kWh: the bill goes to 55–70 kWh — **the box roughly doubles or triples the household's consumption** — and at a marginal 4–6¢ that is **$1.20 to $2.70 a month of new revenue, forever.** Over a ten-year life that's $150–$300 — about what the box cost in 1930. *The utility that financed a $150 refrigerator got the price of the refrigerator back in electricity, and then kept getting it.* And ComEd's terms were thirty-five months: the customer paid off the razor while paying for the blades on the same bill. (Cyrus's phrase; Jeff: "that's the free market being clever.")
- **Collection on the bill (verified):** Under the EHFA plan the dealer "delivers the purchaser's contract to the utility serving the purchaser and the utility pays the dealer the cash sale price… less the down payment" — the utility holds the paper and collects (June 1934). Montana Power, 1934: "$1 down and $1 per month, to be paid with your electric light bill." ComEd, 1935: "charges coming on the electric bill." Tupelo, May 1934: the first TVA-appliance show sold 137 refrigerators and 36 ranges in under two weeks, and "residential consumption in Tupelo jumped 10,000 kilowatt hours in April." The "electricity promised larger profits" line on electric-over-gas (NCPH/Rees) could not be re-fetched — keep as in overview `[VERIFY]`.
- **Scale of the federal program, honestly:** Field (BHR 1990) counts about **4,886 EHFA contracts** through June 1935 and calls the "70,000 refrigerators in 1934" figure in circulation "greatly inflated." The EHFA mattered as a model and a price signal ($80 box), not as volume. `[BELIEVABILITY]` — don't say "the government financed America's refrigerators"; say "showed the utilities how."

#### The adoption curve, anchored the way Jeff asked

- **Refrigerator (U.S. households):** 4.6% of all homes (April 1929, Tobey) / ~8% (1930, overview) → **44.1% mechanical (1940 census — verified)** → ~80% (1950, overview `[VERIFY]`). 1940 detail worth saying: **27.1% still ice; 27.4% none** — "in 1940, more than one American home in four had nothing — not a refrigerator, not an icebox." Urban homes: 56% mechanical. Southern homes: 31% mechanical, 40% none. Nonwhite households: under 10% mechanical, half with nothing.
- **Radio:** 19% of homes (1925, RCA) → **40% (1930 census)** → 83% (1940 census) ([Wikipedia, Golden Age of Radio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Age_of_Radio)). About the same pace as the refrigerator, a decade earlier.
- **Television:** 2% (1949) → 8% (1950) → 59% (1954) ([Paleofuture](https://paleofuture.com/blog/2014/4/21/no-tech-adoption-is-not-speeding-up-in-the-21st-century), citing Pew/FCC). Faster.
- **Telephone — correction to the blueprint:** 45% of households in **1945**, 75% by 1957 ([Wikipedia, citing NYT 1995 and Putnam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_telephone_in_the_United_States)). Bell's patent was 1876: the telephone took roughly **seventy years** to reach half of homes, not fifty. Say "the telephone didn't reach half of American homes until after the Second World War."
- **Electricity:** 8% (1907) → 35% (1920) → 68% (1929; 85% excluding farms) (Paleofuture).
- ⭐ **Suggested line, believable and anchored:** "The refrigerator went from one home in twenty to nearly one in two during the 1930s — about the pace of radio, slower than television, and faster than the telephone ever managed — and it did it in the worst decade in American economic history." Jeff's disbelief from the meeting is the right reaction; the answer is deflation, the sealed box, the safe gas, and thirty-five months on the light bill.

#### `[MISSING PERSPECTIVE]` — the women who ran the kitchens

- No first-person account of a woman deciding to buy an electric refrigerator surfaced. The Library of Congress WPA life histories were unreachable; Twilley and Rees could not be fetched.
- **What is documented:** Shelley Nickles, "'Preserving Women': Refrigerator Design as Social Process in the 1930s," *Technology and Culture* 43:4 (Oct 2002), 693–727 (citation verified via Crossref; text paywalled). From training data, her argument: mid-1930s market research (GE, Sears' Coldspot, Westinghouse) found working-class women wanted bigger, showier boxes with more storage than the restrained "streamline" taste of designers and the affluent, and refrigerator design bent toward them — `[VERIFY]`. Ruth Schwartz Cowan, *More Work for Mother* (1983): appliances raised standards rather than cut hours; the refrigerator's real gift was fewer shopping trips — training data, `[VERIFY]`.
- **Period evidence of who decided:** ComEd's Reace, 1935: "the husband and wife factor is essential, and evening is the time to reach the combination" — salesmen worked three evenings a week because that was when both were home. The savings worksheet is addressed to the housewife's ledger. Nebraska Power's salesmen were "graduates of [cooking] school" and cooked lunch for "Friend Husband" while he sat and smoked (June 1934).
- Per Jeff: the script does not announce the gap. It doesn't ventriloquize either — the trade press shows the sale was made to her, in her kitchen, in the evening.

### Consequences & Changes

- **Daily marketing → the weekly shop:** the 1935 worksheet's own words — "at least a week's supply instead of one day or one meal at a time." The iceman's card in the window, the drip pan, the wagon — gone from big cities within about a decade of 1940 (see above; no single date is sourced).
- **Frozen food (one line):** Birdseye's first retail test, **March 6, 1930, Springfield, Massachusetts**, twenty-seven products "from vegetables and fruits to fish and meats" ([Mass Moments](https://www.massmoments.org/moment-details/clarence-birdseye-tests-frozen-food.html) — note 27, not the overview's 26); Postum/Goldman's Frosted Foods had bought Birdseye for $22 million in June 1929.
- **Supermarkets (one line):** King Kullen opened **August 4, 1930**, 171-06 Jamaica Avenue, Queens — ten times the size of an A&P, self-service, cash, parking ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Kullen)).
- **Winners:** GE (a million Monitor-Tops), Frigidaire/GM, Kelvinator, Westinghouse; DuPont via Kinetic Chemicals (planted for Wave 4 — DuPont did not merely license Freon; it co-owned the maker with GM from 1930); the utilities; the household with a wire. **Losers:** the ice plants and icemen; Einstein and Szilárd as inventors (forty-five-plus filings, $750 from Electrolux, no product — though the money funded the chain reaction); the dead of the methyl-chloride years.
- **Second-order:** the 1940 census map of who had cold and who didn't — urban 56%, the South 31%, nonwhite households 10% — is the first draft of Wave 4's cooling gap.

### Road Not Taken — the gas-flame kitchen (the Einstein anchor *is* this field)

- **Who bet on it:** Carré (1859); Platen and Munters (Electrolux, 1920s); Servel in America (1926–1956); AEG and the two most famous physicists in Berlin.
- **Why compression won:** the grid made a motor cheap; Steenstrup's dome ended the leaks mechanically; Freon ended the danger chemically — "promised to eliminate the danger of leaks" (Dannen). Absorption's efficiency was lower (Korodi's own calculation; 2.3 kWh/day for the prototype).
- **The other world:** kitchens with a pilot flame and no compressor hum — quieter, no CFCs — but tied to gas lines, hungrier for energy, carrying their own carbon-monoxide hazard (CPSC's 1998 Servel warning), and, on the evidence of Servel's thirty years, slower to reach everyone. Jeff's line stands: the better answer had real costs too. Do not lean toward "we could have prevented climate change."

### Bridge to Next Chapter

By 1940 nearly half of American kitchens hum; by 1950, four in five. The gas inside is the compound Kettering called "the only such compounds known which are stable, non-inflammable, and completely nontoxic." Freon passed every test the engineers wrote: the right boiling point, carries heat, doesn't corrode, doesn't burn, doesn't kill. It failed one test nobody had written — because the property that made it safe, its inertness, is the property that lets it last: CFCs are "not destroyed in the lower atmosphere" and take "as long as two to five years" to drift up into the stratosphere, where ultraviolet light finally breaks them and "one chlorine atom can destroy over 100,000 ozone molecules" ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/basic-ozone-layer-science)); their "lack of reactivity gives them a lifespan that can exceed 100 years" ([Wikipedia, CFC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chlorofluorocarbon)). A few thousand boxes never touch the sky; eighty percent of American kitchens does. Midgley's candle is the hinge — tell it here as relief; pick it up next as the problem. (Dannen's own aside, 1997: "Only decades later, of course, would it be realized that such chlorofluorocarbons might endanger the ozone layer of the entire planet.")

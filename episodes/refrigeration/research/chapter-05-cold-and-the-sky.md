## Deep Research

### Narrative Arc

The wave opens on a death and ends on a treaty about refrigerators that is, by itself, one of the largest climate actions ever taken. In between: the safest chemical ever put in a kitchen turns out to be *too* safe — so inert that nothing on Earth destroys it, so it drifts for decades until it reaches the one place with light hard enough to break it. Two chemists in Irvine work out the consequence in three months and are treated as cranks for a decade. Consumers move before governments do. Europe suspects an American trick. Then three men at a British ice-shelf station, with an instrument design from the 1920s and a monitoring program on the chopping block, find a hole in the sky that the satellites had already flagged but nobody had cross-checked. Two years later, twenty-four nations sign in Montreal; six months after that, the company that invented the gas reverses itself in twenty days. The sky begins to heal — on a schedule measured in decades. And the replacement gas turns out to warm the planet, so the world signs again, in Kigali. The pattern the whole episode has been tracing — today's fix sows the seeds of tomorrow's problem, and it's *scale* that turns a harmless fix into a planetary one — finally reaches the sky, and for once the world fixes it together.

### Anchor Story 1 (fully developed): "It looks like the end of the world" — Irvine, winter 1973–74

- **Who:** Mario Molina (30), Mexican-born postdoc, three months into a new job; F. Sherwood "Sherry" Rowland (46), his professor; Joan Rowland, Sherry's wife of ~21 years.
- **When:** Molina arrives fall 1973; the theory is complete around December 1973; the paper appears in *Nature* **June 28, 1974**; the call for a ban comes at the ACS meeting in Atlantic City, **September 1974**; DuPont's full-page ad, **1975** (dated June 30, 1975 by ACS/overview — `[VERIFY]` date).
- **Where:** Rowland's lab at UC Irvine; the Rowland home (Corona del Mar, per Cagin & Dray).
- **Setup:** Rowland had heard, at an Atomic Energy Commission meeting in 1972, "a presentation about recent measurements by the English scientist, Jim Lovelock, of the atmospheric concentrations of a trace species, the man-made chlorofluorocarbon CCl₃F." Lovelock had found the stuff *everywhere*, at levels suggesting essentially all the CFC ever made was still in the air. Rowland: "I knew that such a molecule could not remain inert in the atmosphere forever, if only because solar photochemistry at high altitudes would break it down." He put the question on his list of possible postdoc projects. (Source: [Rowland Nobel autobiography](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1995/rowland/biographical/).)
- **Molina's choice (his words):** He had done a Berkeley Ph.D. (1972) on chemical lasers and "was dismayed by the fact that high-power chemical lasers were being developed elsewhere as weapons"; he "wanted to be involved with research that was useful to society, but not for potentially harmful purposes." At Irvine, "The one project that intrigued me the most consisted of finding out the environmental fate of certain very inert industrial chemicals – the chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs)… This project offered me the opportunity to learn a new field – atmospheric chemistry – about which I knew very little." Wikipedia paraphrases his framing as: "What is the consequence of society releasing something to the environment that wasn't there before?" (Sources: [Molina Nobel autobiography](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1995/molina/biographical/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Molina).) `[FLAG]` The blueprint's phrasing "because nobody knew where the molecules went" is a fair paraphrase of "finding out the environmental fate" — but it is not a quotation. Don't put it in quotation marks.
- **What happened (the chemistry, for Cyrus):** The molecules do nothing in the lower atmosphere — they don't burn, dissolve, or react — so they drift upward over years. In the stratosphere, above most of the ozone, they finally meet ultraviolet light hard enough to knock a chlorine atom loose (photolysis). That chlorine atom hits an ozone molecule: **Cl + O₃ → ClO + O₂**. The ClO then hits a free oxygen atom: **ClO + O → Cl + O₂**. The chlorine atom is back, unchanged, ready to go again. It's a *catalyst* — not consumed. EPA: "One chlorine atom can destroy over 100,000 ozone molecules before it is removed from the stratosphere." The paper's abstract: the compounds "are chemically inert and may remain in the atmosphere for 40–150 years, and concentrations can be expected to reach 10 to 30 times present levels." (Sources: [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/basic-ozone-layer-science); [Molina & Rowland, Nature 249, 810 (28 June 1974)](https://www.nature.com/articles/249810a0).) **The scale point for the hosts' thesis:** the 40–150-year lifetime means the atmosphere is a *cumulative* account. A few thousand Freon fridges in 1932 never mattered. Eighty percent of American kitchens plus every car A/C plus every spray can, for forty years, did — and the bill was still arriving decades after the last can.
- **"Three months":** Molina: "Three months after I arrived at Irvine, Sherry and I developed the 'CFC-ozone depletion theory.'" Rowland: "Within three months, Mario and I realized that this was not just a scientific question, challenging and interesting to us, but a potentially grave environmental problem involving substantial depletion of the stratospheric ozone layer." Molina later: "We really sort of stumbled onto a problem of global proportions." (Sources: both Nobel autobiographies; Visionlearning.)
- **The kitchen line:** Best-attested wording — **"The work is going well, but it looks like the end of the world."** (Roan, *Ozone Crisis*, 1989; repeated in Bailey, Adler, Duden, CQ *Editorial Research Reports*.) Visionlearning's variant: "It's going very well. It just means, I think, the end of the world." Use the Roan version; attribute to Joan's recollection. Then: "she searched the house and threw out every spray can" (UCI). ⭐
- **Atlantic City, September 1974:** At the ACS national meeting they presented a 150-page report and called for "a complete ban on further releases of CFCs into the atmosphere." (Source: [Wikipedia, Molina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Molina).) `[VERIFY]` exact phrasing; the ACS landmark page would not load this pass.
- **The response (honest version):** Rowland's Nobel bio: the problem "became a highly visible public concern in late 1974, and brought with it… legislative hearings, extensive media coverage." Visionlearning: "severe backlash from industry. Even the scientific community was skeptical" — "an absence of speaking invitations for the entire decade after the *Nature* article was published." Robert Abplanalp, inventor of the aerosol valve, complained to UCI's chancellor about Rowland's public statements (Wikipedia, Ozone depletion). DuPont's chairman, July 1975: the theory was "a science fiction tale… a load of rubbish… utter nonsense" (Chemical Week, July 16, 1975, via Energy Probe). And **the 1975 full-page ad** — the verified sentence: **"Should reputable evidence show that some fluorocarbons cause a health hazard through depletion of the ozone layer, we are prepared to stop production of the offending compounds."** (Sources: [Energy Probe](https://ep.probeinternational.org/1997/09/01/dupont-case-study-3d-corporate-strategy/); Cogan 1988 p. 166; Roan 1989 p. 298.)
- **Why "defensible in 1975" is true (Jeff's beat):** In 1975 there was no measurement of ozone loss anywhere — the theory rested on laboratory rate constants and a one-dimensional model. Morrisette: the NAS study of September 1976 "supported Molina and Rowland's theory" but recommended waiting "a period of further study of no more than two years" before regulating. DuPont's pledge was conditional on evidence that did not yet exist. **What changed was the evidence** — 1985 (the hole), 1986–87 (Solomon; NOZE), March 1988 (Ozone Trends Panel). Then DuPont kept the pledge in twenty days. The skeptics weren't corrupt; they were early — and then, briefly, late.
- **Aftermath:** Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1995 — Molina, Rowland, Crutzen. Rowland in 1997: "Is it enough for a scientist simply to publish a paper?… If not us, who? If not now, when?" (UCI). The widely quoted line **"What's the use of having developed a science well enough to make predictions if, in the end, all we're willing to do is stand around and wait for them to come true?"** is attributed to Rowland in Cagin & Dray's *Between Earth and Sky* (1993, p. 464), Gribbin & Kelly's *Winds of Change*, Somerville's *The Forgiving Air*, Flannery, Griffin — `[VERIFY]` the original occasion (I believe a 1986 *New Yorker* profile by Paul Brodeur, but could not confirm). Safe to use as "Rowland liked to ask…" ⭐
- **Why it matters:** The discovery happens in a kitchen — the room the gas was invented for. And it sets up the honest reading of the resister.
- **Sources:** Nobel autobiographies (both); UCI obituary; Visionlearning; Nature 249 abstract; Energy Probe; Morrisette; Open Library full-text hits (Roan; Cagin & Dray).

### The decade of stalemate (1975–85) (~2 min)

- ⭐ **Consumers moved first.** Morrisette: "The fear of skin cancer from the depletion of stratospheric ozone due to the use of CFCs as aerosol propellants in spray cans personalized the risks for many people… The public came to view the risks of using CFC-based aerosols as unacceptable. As a result, **even before the aerosol ban of 1978, the sale of aerosol products fell sharply. Industry, feeling invulnerable, was not prepared for such a strong public and political reaction.**" (Source: [Morrisette 1989](https://courses.seas.harvard.edu/climate/eli/Courses/EPS281r/Sources/Ozone-hole/more/Policy-Responses-to-Ozone-Depletion.pdf), p. 800.) *Cyrus's point:* culture moved faster than regulation — hairspray was nonessential, and there was a pump bottle on the shelf next to it. Substitutes on the shelf is the recurring condition.
- **The U.S. aerosol ban.** IMOS task force (Jan 1975); NAS report (Sept 1976); Toxic Substances Control Act (Oct 1976); EPA proposed rules May 1977; **"in March 1978 final regulations banning the nonessential use of CFCs in aerosols were promulgated by EPA and FDA… The ban took effect in December 1978."** Canada, Sweden, Norway, Denmark also banned aerosol CFCs; the U.S. was "the only major producer/user to do so." (Morrisette, pp. 801–802.) Morrisette's own verdict: "It had taken less than five years to move from the scientific discovery… to the implementation of a major new regulation." — that is the "collective action" story's first act, and it was domestic.
- **Why nothing happened on refrigerants:** "other uses of CFCs, such as refrigeration, could not easily be described as nonessential, and the argument for readily available substitutes for non-aerosol uses of CFCs was not as strong. In addition, the chemical industry, reeling from the aerosol defeat, persuasively argued that time was needed to develop substitutes." (Morrisette, p. 802.) — Note for the thesis: *the fridge was the hard case.* Nobody was going to give up the box.
- **Europe balks — and why it was reasonable.** "The greatest resistance to CFC regulations came from France and Britain, both major producers and users of CFCs." Downing and Kates: their reluctance owed to "the potential economic impact, the nature of their environmental decisionmaking process, and the fact that they were involved in developing a commercial SST." **"With the memory of the Concorde still fresh, the British and French were skeptical of U.S. motivations"** — they had watched America kill its own SST program partly on ozone grounds and then fight Concorde's landing rights, and "were angered by what they saw as U.S. 'environmental neocolonialism.'" (Morrisette, pp. 797–798, 803.) The EEC took a 30% aerosol cut that was "mostly symbolic, since aerosol use in Europe had already declined significantly."
- **Industry regroups:** 1980 — DuPont "initiated formation of the Alliance for Responsible CFC Policy," an industry lobby (Energy Probe). 1979 DuPont position: "No ozone depletion has ever been detected… all ozone depletion figures to date are based on a series of uncertain projections." (Energy Probe.) — *Which was true.*
- **Vienna, March 1985 — framework only.** The Vienna Convention, "adopted at a conference of 43 states in March 1985," committed states to protect "human health and the environment against adverse effects resulting or likely to result from human activities which modify or are likely to modify the ozone layer" — an "umbrella treaty" with no control measures. The protocol failed over a dispute between the "Toronto Group" (U.S., Canada, Sweden, Norway, Finland — who wanted an 80% cut or ban on aerosol use) and the EEC (a production-capacity cap plus 30% aerosol cut — while "only producing at 65 percent of capacity"). (Morrisette, pp. 804–805.) **Two months later, Farman's paper appeared.**

### Anchor Story 2 (fully developed — the wave's climax): The graph that was supposed to reassure everyone — Cambridge and Halley, 1977–85

- **Who:** Jonathan Shanklin (24 when hired), Joe Farman (senior physicist, at Halley since 1957), Brian Gardiner (data quality); the NASA Goddard Ozone Processing Team (Fleig, Heath, Bhartia; Stolarski).
- **When:** Shanklin joins BAS 1977; open day 1983; the "minimum 11-day mean" plot 1984; *Nature* **16 May 1985** (vol. 315, pp. 207–210; the online record shows "01 May 1985," the issue date is 16 May). NASA's continent-scale map: *Nature*, **28 August 1986**.
- **Where:** British Antarctic Survey, Cambridge; Halley Research Station on the Brunt Ice Shelf, Antarctica.
- **Setup — the instrument and the program:** The Dobson spectrophotometer is a 1920s design; Farman "deployed instruments… including a Dobson meter, for measuring ozone" at Halley in 1957, during the International Geophysical Year. Halley's ozone monitoring "wasn't originally intended for monitoring long-term ozone changes: it was set up to help improve weather forecasting and verify theories of atmospheric circulation." And: **"In the early 1980s, the BAS was looking at ways to economize, and the ozone monitoring at Halley was in the frame to be cut; nothing seemed to be changing, and there seemed little reason to keep it going."** (Sources: [Wikipedia, Farman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Farman); [Shanklin 2010](https://acd-ext.gsfc.nasa.gov/People/Stolarski/history_files/465034a.pdf).) `[FLAG]` The *Guardian* obituary says Thatcher — "herself a former chemist" — protected BAS's overall budget after the Falklands by "ringfencing its budget." Both can be true: the Survey survived; the ozone line item was still marked for cutting. Don't overclaim Thatcher.
- **The hire:** Job ad: "Wanted: physicist with an interest in meteorology and programming skills." Shanklin: "In 1977, I joined the BAS in Cambridge, UK, as a recent graduate in natural sciences from the University of Cambridge… **I had no background in meteorology, and no preconceived ideas of how the atmosphere behaved.** The prevailing expectation at the time was that chlorine from CFCs would affect the ozone layer… most marked high in the tropical stratosphere… No one was searching for long-term patterns in springtime data for the Antarctic. But I did not know any of this." (UKRI; Shanklin 2010.) ⭐
- **The backlog:** "One of my tasks at the BAS was to write computer programs to process observations made with our manual Dobson ozone spectrophotometers… At that time, researchers had to use slide rules or log tables to convert Dobson measurements into the amount of ozone in the column of air above. Needless to say, a backlog was building up. I supervised staff as they digitized the handwritten data sheets, and wrote a suite of codes to compute instrument calibrations and then calculate ozone amounts. **By chance, the backlog covered the crucial decade when ozone levels began to drop.**" (Shanklin 2010.) ⭐
- **The open day (1983) — the graph meant to reassure:** "I was also asked to help out with an 'open day' planned for the BAS in 1983, in part because I have a physics-teaching qualification. The popular press was reporting at the time on studies suggesting that aerosol spray cans and exhaust gases from Concorde flights could destroy the ozone layer. Models showed, however, that the expected loss of ozone thus far was only a few per cent. **I wanted to reassure the public by showing that our ozone data from that year were no different from 20 years earlier. The graph we presented to the public showed that no significant change in ozone had been detected over the years, which was true overall — but it seemed that the springtime values did look lower from one year to the next.**" (Shanklin 2010.) In his 2025 voice: "Being an ignorant physicist, I thought this unlikely, so decided to present that year's data and compare it with values my boss had computed from a decade earlier" (UKRI); "And me, being a naive young scientist, thought what? No rubbish. That's highly unlikely, isn't it?" (BAS 2025). ⭐⭐
- **No eureka:** "As I remember it, there was no real eureka moment in the discovery, more a combination of pieces falling into place. Comments from observers at our Antarctic stations suggested that on occasion they saw unusually low ozone values in the spring. The graphs compiled for the open day weren't in themselves convincing, because the prevailing theory suggested that springtime values were highly variable and dependent on short-term weather conditions." (Shanklin 2010.) 2025: **"The problem was the points were starting to fall off the top of the graph. Quite unusual in science, really…"** (BAS 2025.) ⭐
- **The plot that convinced them:** "**What convinced the team was a graph plotting the minimum 11-day mean, which clearly showed that the spring decline was systematic.** Farman crucially developed a chemical theory to explain the observations, linking them to rises in CFCs, and Gardiner carried out the essential quality control on the data. I was a minor player in the end result; my persistence in looking at the data was my real contribution." (Shanklin 2010.) 2025: "In the end, I plotted up all the data and was able to show that the springtime ozone lines over Halley Station were getting systematically lower year by year… And it was that that told an unsuspecting world that there was a link between the declining ozone over Antarctica and the increasing chlorofluorocarbons in the atmosphere." 2019 (Nature PastCast): "I think I was probably the little thing in the background that made the difference." Farman's side (Guardian): "he thought the instrument must be wrong" — a replacement Dobson gave the same readings.
- **The paper's famous figure:** "we flipped the axis for the CFCs (with high concentrations, unusually, at the bottom, and low concentrations at the top) and adjusted the scale. This resulted in a dramatic figure… although I was slightly surprised that we were allowed to present it this way." Title: "Large losses of total ozone in Antarctica reveal seasonal ClOx/NOx interaction," *Nature* 315, 207–210. Halley's October values were ~30% lower in 1980–84 than 1957–73 (Solomon et al. 1986, summarizing); the popular figure is "40%" (Morrisette: "Data from 1984 indicated as much as a 40 percent loss of ozone as compared to measurements taken 20 years earlier").
- **Why Halley, of all places:** "First, it had a continuous ozone record going back to the International Geophysical Year of 1957–58, whereas others, for example the Japanese Syowa station, had only patchy records. The relatively northern latitude of Halley meant that we could begin observations earlier in the spring than at the South Pole, and so see the lowest levels of ozone. In addition, the centre of the ozone hole is often offset towards the Atlantic Ocean, giving Halley lower ozone values than would be visible from stations on the Pacific side." (Shanklin 2010.)
- ⭐⭐ **The letter — and Jeff's question, answered accurately.** Shanklin: "There has been much discussion about how we managed to report the hole before the researchers looking at satellite data. In the years running up to the discovery, we were making ground-based measurements to coincide with satellite overpasses, and communicating them to the Satellite Ozone Analysis Center at the University of California in Livermore. **After the 1983 open day I wrote to the head of the centre, asking whether the satellites were confirming some low-ozone values. I never received a reply — perhaps another lucky break for our team.** I don't know what happened behind the scenes with the satellite teams, but I do know that they were overwhelmed by large amounts of data. One of their goals was to look at latitudinal variation in ozone, which probably involved averaging ozone values around a latitude circle. This sort of analysis would have effectively hidden the ozone hole, because of its offset from the pole." (Shanklin 2010.)
  **What NASA actually did (RealClimate, Gavin Schmidt, Dec 2017, from Stolarski's account):** "It *is* true that the Quality Control (QC) flag on the retrieval was set whenever the inferred ozone level dropped below 180 Dobson Units" — flagged, **not deleted**; the data stayed in the record. "It wasn't however true that no-one at NASA had noticed." In **August 1984**, the Ozone Processing Team at Goddard (A. Fleig, Donald Heath, P.K. Bhartia) was processing the October 1983 data and "the large increase in flagged data… in October 1983 was noticed and investigated." The obvious check was the South Pole station's own Dobson readings — but "the preliminary observations from Oct–Dec 1983 had been invalid – the wrong channels had been read on the instrument." Meanwhile "retrieved values from the rest of the world outside of the polar vortex were normal so the puzzle remained." **"By December 1984, the OPT team was confident enough that the data was real that they submitted an abstract for a conference to be held in Prague in August 1985."** They had already generated a map of the October 1983 hole. "However, before this had been publicly presented, the Farman et al paper was published in *Nature* in May 1985." Schmidt's conclusion: **"It isn't a simple tale of over-confidence in algorithms in the face of black swan events, but rather a tale of poor communications and siloed researchers that slowed down the ability of the wider community to see and interpret what was going on."** (Source: [RealClimate](https://www.realclimate.org/index.php/archives/2017/12/what-did-nasa-know-and-when-did-they-know-it/).) 
  **Debunk, gently:** The version in Farman's own *Guardian* obituary — NASA "failed to report it because its software was set to ignore such unusual readings" — is the myth. The software *flagged*; humans were checking; the check failed for a mundane reason (wrong channels read on the ground instrument); and the British team on the ground got to print first. **Siloes, not stupidity. Answer for Jeff:** somebody *was* following up. They were fourteen months behind three men with a slide-rule-era instrument and no theory to protect — and the ground truth they needed was itself broken. `[FLAG]` The blueprint's phrase "erroneous and uncorrectable" is not the RealClimate wording; use "the wrong channels had been read on the instrument."
- **Then NASA confirmed it, bigger:** Stolarski, Krueger, Schoeberl, McPeters, Newman & Alpert, "Nimbus 7 satellite measurements of the springtime Antarctic ozone decrease," *Nature* 322, 808–811 (28 Aug 1986): the decrease "occurs during September as the Sun rises, reaching a minimum in mid-October"; October minima down ~40% over 1979–85, the surrounding maximum zone down ~20% — the hole was continent-sized. (Source: [Nature 322](https://www.nature.com/articles/322808a0).) Stolarski in 2019: "What they found in that paper was, during the springtime, a 40% or more decline" — where models had predicted "a few percent change."
- **Why Antarctica? (one paragraph, Cyrus):** Susan Solomon, 30, with Garcia, Rowland and Wuebbles (*Nature* 321, 755, 19 June 1986): ordinary gas-phase chemistry could not produce the loss; the answer was **heterogeneous** reactions on the ice particles of polar stratospheric clouds — the clouds that form only in the extreme cold of the Antarctic winter — which convert "safe" reservoir chlorine (HCl and ClONO₂) into active forms; when the sun returns in spring, the chlorine is primed. Solomon then led the National Ozone Expedition to McMurdo in 1986 and 1987 (sole leader, only woman) and measured chlorine oxide "100 times higher than expected." Shanklin 2025: "It was all to do with these beautiful clouds that form during the Antarctic winter, where it gets very cold in the centre of the ozone layer…" NASA's Antarctic Airborne Ozone Expedition (1987) flew the ER-2 and DC-8 into the vortex and "confirmed the link between CFCs, chlorine, and ozone loss." (Sources: [Solomon et al. 1986](https://www.nature.com/articles/321755a0); [Wikipedia, Solomon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Solomon); [NASA Ozone Watch](https://ozonewatch.gsfc.nasa.gov/facts/history_SH.html).) *Note the sequence for Morrisette's point below:* the Protocol was signed in September 1987 **before** the cause of the hole was nailed down; the confirming data came out afterward.
- **Aftermath / how it felt (Shanklin's voice):** "I thought it was something that was perhaps peculiar to Antarctica, and only of scientific interest." (UKRI.) "**It is humbling to think that industry across the world has had to change because of what seemed to be a small discovery over an obscure part of Antarctic that most people had never heard of.**" (UKRI.) "The paper really changed the way people look at the environment." (Nature PastCast.) "If we hadn't discovered the hole when we did, and the emission of CFCs had continued, we would have ozone depletion affecting the whole planet giving rise to a big increase in cases of skin cancer and cataracts." (BAS 2015.) ⭐
- **Why it matters:** A monitoring program nobody wanted to pay for, a graph drawn to say "don't worry," and an outsider who kept looking — catching a planetary problem two years before a treaty. And Cyrus's favorite kind of story, told accurately.
- **Sources:** Shanklin, *Nature* 465, 34–35 (6 May 2010) — read in full; BAS 40th anniversary piece (16 May 2025); UKRI/NERC feature; *Nature* PastCast (2019); RealClimate (2017); Stolarski et al. 1986; Solomon et al. 1986; Farman et al. 1985; *Guardian* obituary of Farman (2013); Wikipedia (Farman; Solomon).

### Montreal (Jeff leads) (~2.5 min)

- **The run-up:** By 1986 CFC demand was growing 5% a year and back at mid-1970s levels; EPA had new leadership after Anne Gorsuch resigned (1983); NRDC was suing EPA under the Clean Air Act; and "in 1985 scientists discovered the Antarctic ozone hole, an event which received much media attention." December 1986: protocol talks resume. February 1987: the U.S. proposes a freeze at 1986 levels then a 95% cut in 10–14 years. Europe: "At first they favored only a freeze. However, by February 1987, they agreed to a 20 percent cut, and by that spring there was consensus on the need for a 50 percent reduction" — moved by the science, by their own environmental groups, and because "the EEC countries, as well as Japan, feared that if there was no international agreement, the United States might take unilateral action and impose trade sanctions." (Morrisette, pp. 806–807.)
- ⭐ **The cabinet revolt:** "In the spring of 1987… a serious rift developed within the U.S. administration. Opposition came from within the Office of Management and Budget, and the Departments of Commerce, Energy, and Interior, which supported only a CFC production/use freeze." (Morrisette, p. 807.) The NSA's declassified documents (Feb–July 1987) show the anti-regulation coalition as Interior (Hodel), Commerce, Agriculture, OMB and the Office of Science and Technology Policy, opposing the second-stage 30% cut, "calling the science into question and warning of economic damage"; State and EPA for a strong protocol; **Shultz** blocking the effort to weaken the position and urging Meese to take it to Reagan directly; **Lee Thomas** defending the strong protocol at a June 18 meeting. (Source: [NSA briefing book](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/climate-change-transparency-project/2024-08-01/ronald-reagan-climate-hero).)
- ⭐ **Hodel's "personal protection plan":** "when Secretary of the Interior Hodel suggested that wearing hats and sunglasses and using sunscreen was an effective way to mitigate the potential health effects of increased UV-B radiation, the story was widely covered by the news media." (Morrisette, p. 807.) *Los Angeles Times*, **May 30, 1987** (Robert Gillette): "Suggests Wearing Hats, Sunscreen Instead of Saving Ozone Layer: Hodel Proposal Irks Environmentalists." Spokesman David Prosperi: it was only "one of several options presented to the President." The press and the movement christened it the **"Rayban Plan"** (attested in Rothman 1998; Frome 1992). (Sources: [Wikipedia, Hodel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_P._Hodel); Open Library full-text.) **Historical-empathy note for Jeff:** adaptation is a real policy category, and Hodel's economists weren't wrong that a 50% cut would cost money; the plan failed the *dread* test, not the arithmetic test — nobody wants to be told the sky is the problem and the answer is a hat.
- **The Senate:** "At one point it appeared as if the U.S. position would be significantly weakened, but the Senate overwhelmingly passed a resolution supporting a 50 percent reduction and eventual phaseout of CFCs, and Hodel's remarks as well as proposals to weaken the U.S. position received much criticism in the press." (Morrisette.) `[VERIFY]` 80–2 / S.Res. 226 / June 1987 — not confirmed; say "almost unanimously."
- ⭐ **Reagan decides — in his own hand:** June 25, 1987 decision memo, handwritten: freeze at 1986 levels; immediate 20% reduction; "a second-phase 30% reduction eight years after the treaty enters force." (NSA.) Morrisette: "Eventually President Reagan's decision was to favor a 50 percent reduction in CFCs and a freeze in halons (the eventual structure of the protocol)… the United States remained the principal advocate for a strong protocol." **The nose:** NSA footnote — "Reagan underwent removal of two skin cancers in 1985 and 1987, and some scholars speculate that this had something to do with his interest in the ozone treaty." No document in the record says so. **Do not say "first head of state."**
- **Montreal, September 16, 1987:** "Representatives from 24 nations, meeting in Montreal in September 1987, signed the 'Montreal Protocol on Substances that Deplete the Ozone Layer.'" (Morrisette, p. 793.) The terms: Group I CFCs (11, 12, 113, 114, 115) frozen at 1986 levels from 1989, cut to 80% by 1994 and 50% by 1999; halons frozen; a ten-year grace period for developing ("Article 5") countries. (Morrisette, pp. 812–813.) `[FLAG]` Wikipedia gives "46 signatories" (those who signed before the signature deadline) and the ACS page says "56 countries"; the September 16 number is 24 + the EEC. Say "two dozen nations."
- **Ratification and Reagan's words:** The Senate ratified unanimously in March 1988 (Morrisette; Reagan's statement gives March 14). Reagan, **April 5, 1988**: "The Montreal protocol is a model of cooperation. It is a product of the recognition and international consensus that ozone depletion is a global problem, both in terms of its causes and its effects… a monumental achievement." (Source: [Reagan Library](https://www.reaganlibrary.gov/archives/speech/statement-signing-montreal-protocol-ozone-depleting-substances).) Transmittal to the Senate, Dec 21, 1987: "The United States played a leading role in the negotiation of the Protocol." ⭐
- ⭐ **Universal ratification:** "On 16th September 2009, the Vienna Convention and the Montreal Protocol became the first treaties in the history of the United Nations to achieve universal ratification." (UN.) 198 parties today. Kofi Annan: "perhaps the single most successful international agreement to date has been the Montreal Protocol." (Wikipedia.)
- **Thatcher (optional one line):** a chemist by training; the *Guardian* credits her with ringfencing BAS's budget after the Falklands. Her Royal Society speech (Sept 27, 1988) on ozone and climate was not fetched this pass — `[VERIFY]` before quoting.
- ⭐ **Why it worked — two analyses the hosts should hear.** Shanklin (2010): "The discovery of the ozone hole undoubtedly helped to seal those negotiations, but there were several other important drivers to international accord. Chemical manufacturers were able and willing, after some initial resistance, to produce CFC substitutes. The public was keen to see action: the evidence was strong and clear; the hole sounded threatening; and there was a link between thinning ozone and cancer. **And the public did not feel bullied or threatened — no one was telling them to radically change their way of life. There was a problem, and something could be done about it.** By contrast, the evidence for man-made climate change is less clear-cut to the average person. And people are given the impression that civilization will collapse unless they abandon cars and radically change their lives… Not surprisingly, there is confusion and resistance." Also: "**With the ozone hole it was something that was absolutely clear cut, you could show politicians a picture of the Antarctic ozone hole.**" (UKRI.) Morrisette's three factors: "(1) the evolving scientific understanding of the problem, (2) increasing public concern over the problem based on the threat of skin cancer and the discovery of the Antarctic ozone hole, and (3) the availability of acceptable substitutes for CFCs." On the hole specifically: "it was dangerous to use the ozone hole as an argument for the protocol" since its cause wasn't proven in 1987 — "Nevertheless… the ozone hole galvanized world opinion." On substitutes: "DuPont, the world's largest producer of CFCs with 25 percent of the global market" (Morrisette's figure; Energy Probe says ~70% of a $3B market — `[FLAG]` conflicting market-share figures; use "the world's largest producer") had by 1986 called for a worldwide emissions limit and "announced that suitable alternatives could be available within five years given the right market conditions… An international protocol was seen by industry as a useful mechanism for providing the necessary economic incentive to develop and market suitable alternatives." **No substitute, no treaty** — and the treaty *created* the market for the substitute. (Jeff's pro-market beat, honestly earned.)

### DuPont's twenty days — March 1988 (Jeff leads) (~2 min)

- **March 4, 1988 — Heckert's letter.** DuPont chairman Richard E. Heckert to U.S. senators: **"scientific evidence does not point to the need for dramatic CFC emission reductions. There is no available measure of the contribution of CFCs to any observed ozone change…"** (Source: [Energy Probe](https://ep.probeinternational.org/1997/09/01/dupont-case-study-3d-corporate-strategy/).) 1987 congressional testimony, for context: "we believe that there is no immediate crisis that demands unilateral regulation." `[VERIFY]` recipients (Baucus, Durenberger, Stafford per training) — say "three senators."
- **March 15, 1988 — the Ozone Trends Panel.** Convened by NASA in December 1986 under Robert Watson, with ten working groups (Grundmann; Vancil). Its finding, per Morrisette: "using ground-based measurements for the period 1969–1986, [it] has reported a global reduction of between **1.7 and 3.0 percent** in the northern hemisphere for latitudes between 30 and 64 degrees… Decreases, however, have been as great as **6.2 percent** during the winter months. The Ozone Trends Panel has attributed this decrease to CFCs and other atmospheric trace gases." (Morrisette, pp. 795–796.) — i.e., not just Antarctica: **over the northern mid-latitudes, where the people live**, and not explainable by natural cycles. `[VERIFY]` "100+ scientists" (training; consistent with the panel's 18-station reanalysis and multinational authorship). **Believability:** "1.7 to 3 percent" sounds small; the anchor is that models had predicted essentially zero by 1986, and 6% in winter was the direction of the Antarctic curve.
- ⭐ **The inside man.** Mack McFarland, DuPont's atmospheric scientist, was "the only industry representative on the Ozone Trends Panel" (Litfin); DuPont had "named Mack McFarland to keep them abreast of latest scientific findings" (Nance). He had seen the draft. Roan (*Ozone Crisis*, 1989) puts him on the phone that week: "Mario Molina took a phone call from McFarland that Wednesday. McFarland wanted Molina to hear…" (the snippet ends). The account that he then briefed Wilmington that the link was verified comes from the HBS case (Vietor & Reinhardt, "Du Pont Freon Products Division (A)," Jan 20, 1989 — abstract: "In 1988, the Du Pont Co. is abruptly confronted with solid scientific evidence that chlorofluorocarbons are destroying the earth's ozone shield") and Mullin's 2002 business-ethics case. **Safe wording:** "DuPont had a scientist *on* the panel. He'd read the draft. He told the company the link was now real." `[VERIFY]` the "phoned the head of government relations" detail before using it.
- **March 24, 1988 — the reversal.** *Washington Post*, Cass Peterson, March 25, 1988: "BAN ON CFCS URGED TO SAVE OZONE SHIELD — DU PONT, TOP MAKER, ASKS TOTAL PHASEOUT: E.I. du Pont de Nemours & Co., the world's leading producer of chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), called yesterday for a total phaseout of the [chemicals]…" (Source: [WaPo](https://www.washingtonpost.com/archive/politics/1988/03/25/ban-on-cfcs-urged-to-save-ozone-shield/f7542c9b-eec4-4ba7-84dc-2a15301f4602/) — headline, byline and lede verified; body paywalled.) ### The encore — HFCs → Kigali (Cyrus leads) (~1.5 min)

- **The fourth fluid.** The substitutes (HCFC-123, HFC-134a) were designed to break down in the troposphere — no chlorine reaching the stratosphere (Morrisette). HFC-134a: **global-warming potential 1,430** times CO₂ over 100 years (EPA's MVAC table, an IPCC AR4 value; AR5 gives ~1,300). For comparison, the CFC-12 it replaced was 10,900. (Source: [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mvac/acceptable-refrigerants-and-their-impacts).) **Believability anchor:** a fridge holds only ~100–150 grams of refrigerant, so a single leak is small — but hundreds of millions of boxes, cars and supermarket racks leaking a few percent a year for decades is a climate line item. *Scale, again.*
- ⭐ **Kigali, October 15, 2016.** 197 countries adopted the Kigali Amendment to the Montreal Protocol in Kigali, Rwanda; in force January 1, 2019. Developed countries cut HFC consumption to 15% of a 2011–13 baseline by 2036; China, Brazil and most developing countries to 20% by 2045; India, Pakistan, the Gulf states and other hot-climate countries to 20% by 2047. WMO/UNEP 2022: compliance "is estimated to avoid **0.3–0.5°C** of warming by 2100." (Sources: [Wikipedia, Kigali Amendment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kigali_Amendment); [WMO/UNEP 2022](https://csl.noaa.gov/assessments/ozone/2022/executivesummary/); [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/ozone-layer-protection/recent-international-developments-under-montreal-protocol).) **A treaty about refrigerants is one of the largest single climate actions ever taken** — and it was done under the *ozone* treaty's machinery, because that machinery worked.
- **The U.S. joins, late and bipartisan:** the AIM Act (Dec 2020) mandated an 85% HFC phasedown by 2036; the Senate consented to Kigali on **September 21, 2022, 69–27** (Roll Call 343; cloture 64–30; a 96–0 amendment insisting China not be treated as developing); ratification deposited Oct 31, 2022. White House: "prevent up to half a degree Celsius of warming this century." (Sources: [Senate roll-call list, 117th Cong. 2nd sess.](https://www.senate.gov/legislative/LIS/roll_call_lists/vote_menu_117_2.htm); [Biden statement](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/briefing-room/statements-releases/2022/09/21/statement-by-president-joe-biden-on-senate-ratification-of-the-kigali-amendment-to-the-montreal-protocol/).) — *Jeff's "could we do it again?" gets a data point: in 2022, yes, 69 votes.*
- **Today's fluids — back to the beginning.** Home refrigerators: **isobutane (R-600a)** — flammable (ASHRAE class A3), so the household appliance standard **IEC/EN 60335-2-24 caps the charge at 150 grams** (Danfoss R600a application guide: "the refrigerant charge must not exceed 150 g"); Secop notes isobutane "has been used in the past in refrigerators up to the 1940s" — it was one of the fluids Freon displaced. Propane (R-290) in small commercial units. **CO₂ (R-744)** in supermarket racks — transcritical systems running at roughly 100 bar `[VERIFY]` (training data). And **ammonia** in industrial cold storage — Linde's fluid, a hundred and fifty years on. (Sources: Danfoss R600a compressor data sheet; Secop "R600a and R290 in small hermetic systems" — both in the Wave 2 research cache.) ⭐ *The hosts' arc in one sentence: four fluids in a hundred years — ammonia, Freon, HFC, and now isobutane-propane-CO₂-ammonia.*
- **Unfinished business (honesty for the optimistic close):** WMO/UNEP 2022 — "Global emissions of long-lived HFC-23… are as much as eight times larger than expected"; "The recent identification of unexpected CFC-11 emissions led to scientific investigations," with reductions since 2018.
- ⭐ **`[GAP]` RESOLVED — the HFO/TFA "forever chemical" debate (for Open Question 3):** The fifth-generation fluids, hydrofluoroolefins (e.g., **HFO-1234yf**, now in most new car A/C under the EU MAC directive's GWP<150 rule), have a GWP below 1 and an atmospheric lifetime of days. The catch: HFO-1234yf "degrades to trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), which may accumulate in water." TFA is an ultrashort-chain PFAS — "the most abundant PFAS found in the environment" — extremely persistent and so water-soluble that it moves everywhere. Germany's environment agency estimates refrigerants contribute ~1,170 tonnes of TFA a year in Germany vs. ~500 from pesticides; German and Swiss rainfall TFA has risen roughly fivefold since the 1990s; Danish groundwater more than tenfold; TFA "accounted for 98 percent of all PFAS detected in water samples from 10 EU countries." Toxicology moved in 2024–26: Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment asked ECHA to classify TFA as presumed toxic to reproduction; ECHA's committee (June 2026) found it meets criteria for reproductive toxicity category 1B and "persistent, mobile and toxic"; EFSA cut the acceptable daily intake 72% (July 2026). Removal from drinking water requires reverse osmosis — expensive and wasteful. Industry (EFCTC, the fluorocarbon producers) argues TFA is naturally present in oceans, low in acute toxicity, and that HFOs' climate benefit outweighs it. (Sources: [Wikipedia, TFA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trifluoroacetic_acid) and [HFO-1234yf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2,3,3,3-Tetrafluoropropene), citing UBA, Wang et al. 2018, McGillen et al. 2023, ECHA/EFSA.) **Balanced framing:** the fifth fluid fixed the *sky* problem and may have started a *water* problem — exactly the pattern, smaller and caught earlier. `[VERIFY]` the ECHA/EFSA 2026 items against primary releases before air.

### Consequences & Changes

- **CFCs gone:** complete phase-out 1996 (developed) / 2010 (developing). Atmospheric chlorine and bromine from controlled ODSs "continued to decline" (WMO/UNEP 2022).
- **The sky, honestly:** minimum Antarctic springtime ozone fell ~40% (1975–84) and by 1987 "in excess of 50 percent overall and 95 percent at altitudes between 15 and 20 km"; minima have sat "at about 70% below the late-1970s levels" (Shanklin 2010). Recovery to 1980 values: Antarctic **~2066**, Arctic ~2045, near-global **~2040**; 2019's hole was the smallest since 2002; 2020–21 had large, long-lasting ones. (WMO/UNEP 2022.) Shanklin: "perhaps the most startling lesson from the ozone hole is just how quickly our planet can change." ⭐
- **Winners:** the ozone layer; Molina, Rowland, Crutzen (Nobel 1995); UNEP's treaty machinery (universal ratification 2009); the chemical majors, who sold the substitutes; and — Shanklin's "happy accident" — the climate: "the Montreal Protocol has done more to reduce greenhouse-gas emissions than the Kyoto treaty — CFCs are potent greenhouse gases." **Losers:** the CFC franchise (by choice); the people who got the skin cancers from depletion that already happened; the billion without cold — and, if the TFA critics are right, the water.
- **Jeff's line from the comments, verified as fair:** "a remarkable story of collective global action that seems incomprehensible in today's environment" — the record supports every word, with one footnote: in 2022 the U.S. Senate did it again, 69–27.

### Road Not Taken (concrete and sourced): sunglasses instead of a treaty

- **The alternative:** adaptation — Hodel's hats, sunglasses and sunscreen; a production freeze and nothing more (Interior, OMB, Commerce, Energy; the EEC's 1985 position: a capacity cap while producing at 65% of capacity).
- **Who bet on it:** the four departments; the Alliance for Responsible CFC Policy (a "reasonable" global limit on growth of production capacity, fall 1986); France and Britain until spring 1987.
- **Why the treaty won:** dread (skin cancer), a picture (the hole), substitutes five years out, a Senate resolution, trade-sanction fear in Europe and Japan, Shultz taking it to the president, and Reagan's handwritten memo.
- **The other world, from EPA's own model:** under no controls and 2.5%/yr growth through 2050, "over 150 million additional skin cancer cases, resulting in more than three million deaths for the U.S. population born before 2075." **Hedge wording:** "EPA's worst-case model — no controls, sixty more years of growth — projected well over a hundred million extra American skin cancers." And Shanklin's version: "we would have ozone depletion affecting the whole planet." The point isn't the figure; it's that the world chose the harder path and it worked.

### Bridge to Built In

The machine humming in the kitchen has now been through four fluids in a hundred years — Linde's ammonia, Midgley's Freon, the HFCs that replaced it, and now, in a new box, isobutane behind a 150-gram limit, with CO₂ in the supermarket and ammonia back in the warehouse. The sky is healing on a schedule that ends after most listeners are dead. A billion people are where New York was in 1900, and their governments call cold "a developmental need." Montreal worked; Kigali is the encore; the fifth fluid may be a water problem. That's the whole arc — and the numbers land hardest now.

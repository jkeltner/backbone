--- SEGMENT BREAK: Opening - The Welcome ---

JEFF: Hey everybody, welcome to Backbone. I'm Jeff Keltner.

CYRUS: And I'm Cyrus Mistry.

JEFF: This is the show where we tell the messy, contested, slightly mocked history of the technologies that quietly run the modern world. The ones you don't think about until they break.

CYRUS: The stuff that's so obviously there it becomes invisible.

JEFF: Yeah. And today's topic is the cleanest example we're going to do for a long time. Because the technology we're talking about, you literally touch it more than your phone. More than your steering wheel. Probably more than your spouse, depending on the day.

CYRUS: [laughs softly] Probably.

JEFF: And nobody thinks about it.

CYRUS: I'll tell you, reading the prep for this one, I kept stopping every couple paragraphs going, wait — really? And this is the fourth time I've heard a lot of these stories, and I'm still going, what the heck?

JEFF: Right? So. We are talking about refrigeration. And I want to start somewhere that, when I first read about it, I had to literally re-read the page to make sure I had it right.

--- SEGMENT BREAK: Opening - The Cold Open ---

JEFF: It's February tenth, eighteen hundred and six. Boston Harbor. There is a brig at the Custom House called the *Favorite* — a small two-masted ship, just cleared to depart. The owner of the ship is twenty-three years old. He's a Boston Brahmin, son of a judge — a kid who dropped out of school at age thirteen. His name is Frederic Tudor. And he's loading the *Favorite* with one hundred and thirty tons of cargo.

CYRUS: Okay.

JEFF: The cargo is ice.

CYRUS: [laughs] Okay.

JEFF: One hundred and thirty tons of pond ice, cut by hand out of his family's farm pond in Saugus, Massachusetts. Packed in hay and wood shavings. Sitting in the hold of a wooden ship. And the *Favorite* is going to sail this cargo about fifteen hundred miles south... to Martinique. In the Caribbean.

CYRUS: He's shipping ice to the tropics.

JEFF: He's shipping ice to the tropics. And it gets better. Nobody in Boston would charter their ship to him. They thought the ice would melt in the hold and rot the timbers. So he had to buy his own brig. Four thousand seven hundred and fifty dollars. He used most of his inheritance.

CYRUS: Oh my God.

JEFF: Three days after he leaves Boston Harbor, the *Boston Gazette* prints one sentence about the voyage. I want to read it to you verbatim, because two hundred and twenty years later, this line is still the best one-sentence summary of how everybody felt about this idea. Here it is. *"No joke. A vessel has cleared at the Custom House for Martinique with a cargo of ice. We hope this will not prove a slippery speculation."*

CYRUS: [laughs] Slippery speculation. That's good.

JEFF: That is good. That is a 1806 burn. That is somebody at the *Boston Gazette* taking ten seconds out of his day to make sure history remembers that he thought this was the dumbest idea he'd ever heard of.

CYRUS: And then... what happens?

JEFF: He was right. Most of the ice melts before the ship gets to Martinique. Tudor arrives in St. Pierre, and he hasn't built an ice house. He hadn't really thought about what the buyers would do with the ice once they got it. There are no bartenders trained to use ice. There are no household ice boxes. There's no infrastructure to store it. So the ice that did survive the voyage melts on the dock while he's trying to figure out who to sell it to. The voyage loses him four thousand five hundred dollars. In today's money, call it a hundred grand.

CYRUS: Oh my God.

JEFF: He goes home. Within six years, he is in debtor's prison.

CYRUS: Get out.

JEFF: He goes back in twice more before eighteen twenty. Three trips to debtor's prison. And in his journal, during those years, he keeps writing a line — a kind of refrain he comes back to over and over again. *"Let those laugh who win."*

CYRUS: [reflective] Wow.

JEFF: Now — I'm telling you this story for a reason. This is where the entire arc of this episode starts. A twenty-three-year-old kid, mocked in the newspaper, three trips to debtor's prison, shipping frozen pond water to the Caribbean. And by the eighteen eighties, ice from Massachusetts is the second-largest American export by tonnage. Behind only cotton.

CYRUS: Behind only cotton. What the heck?

JEFF: Frozen New England pond water. Behind cotton. That's the story we're telling. And by the way — that *Boston Gazette* line, "slippery speculation"? You're going to hear three more versions of that in this episode. Every breakthrough in this story gets mocked first.

--- SEGMENT BREAK: Opening - By the Numbers ---

CYRUS: Okay. Before we get into Tudor's full arc, let me throw some numbers at you. Because this is where I kept stopping and going, *no way*.

JEFF: Hit me.

CYRUS: The average American household opens their refrigerator door — how many times a day? Just guess.

JEFF: Twenty?

CYRUS: Thirty-three.

JEFF: Thirty-three times a day?

CYRUS: Thirty-three. That's from Energy Star. By the way, I saw a higher figure floating around — somebody claimed a hundred and seven, which is not possible. That's ten openings an hour for ten straight hours. Nobody's living in their kitchen like that. But even thirty-three — the refrigerator is the most-touched object in the modern home.

JEFF: More than the phone?

CYRUS: More than the phone, if you don't count scrolling.

JEFF: That's wild.

CYRUS: Okay, number two. In nineteen thirty, eight percent of American households had a refrigerator. By nineteen fifty — eighty percent.

JEFF: Eight to eighty.

CYRUS: In twenty years. Faster than the smartphone curve. And here's the part that's nuts — the steepest part of that adoption was during the Great Depression.

JEFF: Wait — people are buying refrigerators during the Depression?

CYRUS: During the Depression. We'll get to why later.

JEFF: [reflective] That doesn't fit my mental model of the Depression at all.

CYRUS: A hundred percent. Number three. Refrigeration — globally — uses about twenty percent of the world's electricity. Air conditioning alone uses more electricity than the entire continent of Africa consumes. For everything.

JEFF: AC by itself is bigger than a continent.

CYRUS: It's the cooling backbone. We just don't see it. Now this next one is not a fun fact. It's the cost of the world before refrigeration. In eighteen fifty-eight, in New York City, the *New York Times* estimated that roughly eight thousand infants died in one year — from contaminated milk.

JEFF: [pause] Wait. Eight thousand?

CYRUS: Eight thousand babies. In one city. In one year. More than the entire U-S military death toll in the Mexican-American War.

JEFF: Right. Right.

CYRUS: We're going to get to that — there's a whole scandal around it — but I want to plant the number. The world before refrigeration wasn't quaint. It was lethal in ways we don't really hold in our heads anymore. Okay. Last one. And this one is foreshadowing. One chlorine atom — from one molecule of an old refrigerator coolant — destroys about a hundred thousand ozone molecules in the upper atmosphere.

JEFF: One molecule eats a hundred thousand.

CYRUS: One eats a hundred thousand. The chemistry that made refrigeration safe enough to put in every American kitchen in the nineteen twenties — fifty years later, that same chemistry punched a hole in the sky over Antarctica the size of the United States.

JEFF: I remember being a kid and that being *the* existential crisis everybody was freaking out about.

CYRUS: A hundred percent.

JEFF: We're going to talk about how we solved that, too — because that part is actually one of the more optimistic parts of this whole story.

CYRUS: We did. It's a mind-blowing story when you go back and look at it.

--- SEGMENT BREAK: Opening - The World Before ---

JEFF: Okay. With those numbers in your head, I want you to actually picture life before refrigeration. Because the numbers are abstract. The texture is what gets me. Two images.

CYRUS: Yeah, go.

JEFF: First image. Picture a typical American kitchen, anywhere between eighteen seventy and nineteen twenty. There's a square card hanging in the kitchen window. Cardboard. Four sides. Each side has a number — twenty-five, fifty, seventy-five, one hundred.

CYRUS: Pounds.

JEFF: Pounds of ice. Whichever number's facing the street that morning, that's what you want the iceman to bring. He comes by every day. Horse-drawn wagon. Block of ice on a sheepskin pad on his shoulder. Iron tongs. He doesn't ring the bell — he reads the card, walks the block in the back door, slots it into the icebox.

CYRUS: And the icebox is just a wooden cabinet with a slot for ice on top.

JEFF: Yeah. Zinc-lined. Ice on top, cold air sinks into the food chamber, drip pan underneath catching the meltwater. And here's the part I love — every single night, somebody had to empty that drip pan. Or in the morning the kitchen floor would be flooded.

CYRUS: [laughs softly] Every single night.

JEFF: Every single night. The whole rhythm of the household ran on the iceman. What you could buy, what you could cook, when you had to get to it before it spoiled — all of it ran on his schedule.

CYRUS: It's wild. I'm picturing my kitchen — the way I just throw stuff in the fridge without thinking. That's not a thing that existed for most of human history.

JEFF: Not even a little bit. Second image. The disturbing one. Where that eight-thousand-infants stat comes from. Eighteen fifty-eight, New York City. Enormous demand for fresh milk. But fresh milk cannot survive the trip from rural dairies to urban consumers in any kind of heat. So what do the urban dairymen do? They keep cows in the city.

CYRUS: In the city.

JEFF: Tethered. In basements. Specifically — in basements next to whiskey distilleries, so the cows can eat the spent mash. Free feed. The cows live their entire lives in narrow stalls, standing in their own waste, drinking distillery runoff. The milk that comes out is bluish. Thin. Sometimes blood-flecked. So the dairymen disguise it. Whiten it with plaster of Paris. Thicken it with starch and beaten eggs. Color it with molasses. And they send it out at sunrise.

CYRUS: Holy cow.

JEFF: That's the milk killing the babies. Frank Leslie — publisher of an illustrated newspaper — sends his reporters into the basements in May of eighteen fifty-eight. Multi-issue exposé. The *Times* picks it up. The New York Academy of Medicine investigates. And that's where the eight thousand infant deaths in one year number comes from.

CYRUS: [pause] And that was the system everybody accepted.

JEFF: It was the system everybody accepted. And I want to be careful here — the dairyman wasn't a cartoon villain. He was running the only urban dairy model that worked when fresh milk couldn't survive a hot day. The horror is structural, not personal.

CYRUS: That is such a critical reframe. Looking back, you go, what is wrong with these people? But they're running the only economy that's available to them.

JEFF: Right. New York state passes its first milk-safety regulations four years later — the first U-S food-safety law, forty years before the Pure Food and Drug Act. Refrigeration eventually solves this. But it takes decades. And until it does, this is the world.

CYRUS: Okay. So I want to add something, because I don't want to leave the listener thinking the pre-refrigeration world was just bleak across the board. We have the lack of refrigeration to thank for some of the best food on the planet.

JEFF: [laughs softly] Yeah, go.

CYRUS: Bacon. Pepperoni. Prosciutto. Country ham. Salami. Jamón ibérico. Salt cod. All of that exists because people couldn't refrigerate meat. So they figured out how to cure it. Salt it. Smoke it. Ferment it. Some of the world's greatest cuisines — Italian, Spanish, the whole American South — are basically built on solutions to meat not lasting.

JEFF: I mean, obviously, if you had a refrigerator in ancient Rome, you don't get prosciutto.

CYRUS: You don't get prosciutto.

JEFF: Cato — the Roman senator — wrote a manual on how to dry-cure a ham. Third-century B-C-E pork-preservation guidance.

CYRUS: Some of my favorite things in the world come from the lack of refrigeration. And I think that's a thing the show is going to keep coming back to. Every backbone gives. And every backbone takes away.

JEFF: A backbone gives, and a backbone takes away.

--- SEGMENT BREAK: Opening - The Road Ahead ---

JEFF: Okay. With that picture in mind — the card in the window, the cow in the basement, the bacon — let me tell you what we're doing over the next ninety or so minutes. Three waves. Three distinct chapters. And each one ends with the solution becoming the next problem.

CYRUS: Today's fix sows the seeds of tomorrow's problems. That's the through-line.

JEFF: That's the through-line. Wave one. Cyrus, this is your wave. Cold as Commodity. Eighteen-oh-six to nineteen hundred. The Tudor story all the way through to its absurd conclusion. By the eighteen eighties, the natural ice industry employs ninety thousand people, twenty-five thousand horses, single warehouses on the Hudson holding a quarter of a million tons. Ice is the second-largest American export by tonnage. And then... a heat wave in nineteen hundred kills it. A corrupt monopolist named Charles Morse gouges New York during the heat wave, and the resulting scandal takes down Tammany Hall.

CYRUS: It's such a good arc. And Tudor is the best founder character I've encountered in any of this prep. The guy feels like he would fit in Silicon Valley today. He's a dropout. He's obsessed. He's the original vertical-integration capitalist. He invents the playbook Rockefeller copies fifty years later.

JEFF: And the kicker I love — when his ship the *Tuscany* finally arrives in Calcutta in eighteen thirty-three, after twenty-seven years of grinding — the people in Calcutta had never seen ice. Ever. The Indian merchants think it might grow on trees in America.

CYRUS: [laughs] We're going to do this story justice.

JEFF: That's wave one. Wave two — mine. Cold as Infrastructure. From eighteen thirty-four, when a guy named Jacob Perkins patents the first mechanical refrigerator in London — and can't sell it. Then forty years of failed mechanical inventors. Then, finally, in eighteen seventy-six, a German engineer named Carl von Linde figures out a working ammonia compressor in a Munich brewery. And from there, it explodes. Refrigerated railcars centralize American meat in Chicago. The local butcher trade gets destroyed in a generation. A Chicago oligopoly called the Beef Trust shows up. The federal government invents the modern food safety regime to keep up. A captain named John Whitson nearly freezes to death inside his own ship's hold saving the first frozen-meat shipment from New Zealand to London. And a chemist named Mary Engle Pennington has to apply to the U-S-D-A under her initials — M-E — because the all-male civil service board would never have hired a woman in nineteen-oh-seven.

CYRUS: That story.

JEFF: She's the hidden architect of everything we eat. And almost nobody knows her name. And by the end of wave two, in nineteen twenty-seven, the refrigerator enters the American home. The eight-to-eighty curve happens. During the Depression.

CYRUS: Yeah.

JEFF: That's wave two. Wave three — back to you. Cold as Chemistry. Because at the end of wave two, the refrigerator is entering the home — but the chemicals that make it run are killing people. Methyl chloride. Sulfur dioxide. Ammonia. Families in the nineteen twenties dying in their sleep when the seal leaks overnight. There's a Berlin family in nineteen twenty-six that dies this way. And when Albert Einstein reads about it in the paper, he and Leó Szilárd — two of the most famous physicists of the twentieth century — sit down and try to invent a safer refrigerator.

CYRUS: They actually try.

JEFF: They actually try. And — we'll get to it later — they fail.

CYRUS: Hold that one for later. That's a hell of a teaser.

JEFF: That's a hell of a teaser. Here's what happens instead. A General Motors chemist named Thomas Midgley invents a non-toxic refrigerant in nineteen twenty-eight. Freon. The miracle compound. It works. For fifty years, Freon makes refrigeration safe. Until three British scientists at a research station in Antarctica, in nineteen eighty-five, with a piece of equipment older than they are, find a hole in the sky the size of the United States. And it turns out the chemistry that made our kitchens safe is destroying the atmosphere.

CYRUS: And then the replacement we use to fix the ozone problem turns out to be one of the worst climate gases on the planet. Thousands of times worse than carbon dioxide. So we fix one, we get another.

JEFF: We fix one, we get another. That's wave three. Then we'll do the Backbone Test on the whole thing.

CYRUS: Today's fix sows the seeds of tomorrow's problems.

JEFF: That's the line. And I think it's one of the most useful frames there is for thinking about every technology we live with today, including the one we use to record this podcast.

CYRUS: Yeah.

JEFF: All right. Cyrus, take it away.

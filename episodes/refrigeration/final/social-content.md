## Thread-Style Summary

*(5–7 short posts for X/Twitter or LinkedIn)*

**Post 1:**
In 1806, a 23-year-old Bostonian tried to ship pond ice to the Caribbean. The Boston Gazette called it "a slippery speculation." He died worth ~$200M in today's money. That pattern — world laughs, world becomes dependent — is the story of refrigeration. And it happened three times.

**Post 2:**
The man who *actually* invented the ice machine — the principle behind every fridge on Earth — was a doctor in Apalachicola, Florida named John Gorrie. He was right. The technology worked. The northern ice industry destroyed him in the press, his backer died, and he retreated into seclusion at 51. His statue stands in the US Capitol.

**Post 3:**
When the natural ice trade finally won the market, it built empires. 90,000 workers. 25,000 horses. Second only to cotton in American shipping tonnage. And then one warm winter in 1889 — the Hudson River didn't freeze. The whole premise collapsed. Nobody wrote down what happened to those 90,000 workers.

**Post 4:**
In April 1930, a chemist named Thomas Midgley took the stage at the American Chemical Society meeting in Atlanta. He inhaled a new chemical called Freon — on stage, in front of the whole room — and exhaled onto a candle. The flame went out. Two things proved simultaneously: non-toxic, non-flammable. Home refrigerators became safe. Within 5 years: 8 million units sold. The ozone hole is confirmed in 1985.

**Post 5:**
Frederick McKinley Jones: orphaned at 9, sixth-grade education, self-taught mechanic, then self-taught electronics, then self-taught refrigeration. In 1938, his business partner bet $6 on a golf course that Jones could solve truck refrigeration in 30 days. Jones went to the library. He built Thermo King. His units kept blood plasma cold on WWII battlefields. First Black American to win the National Medal of Technology — awarded posthumously, 30 years after he died.

**Post 6:**
Here's the thing about refrigeration that keeps getting me: every wave of this story had resisters who were RIGHT about the risks. The ship captains were right that ice was impractical. The butchers were right that Chicago beef was unverifiable. The public was right not to trust an industry that had lied about refrigerant safety. The resisters were right about the immediate problem. They just couldn't stop the dependency once it formed.

**Post 7:**
In February 2021, Texas families stored food in snowbanks outside their houses. Two hundred years after Tudor, the most technologically advanced state in the richest country on Earth went back to using nature's cold — because the machines stopped. That's what "built in" actually means. Not built into the technology. Built into civilization.

## Thread-Style Summary

Seven posts. Works as an X thread or a LinkedIn carousel script.

**1/** February 1806. A brig clears Boston Harbor for Martinique carrying eighty tons of pond ice. The Boston Gazette: "We hope this will not prove a slippery speculation." The owner is 23, left school at 13, and his own brother thinks he's lazy. Within a few years he's in debtors' prison. His motto: "Let those laugh who win."

**2/** He won. Sawdust — sawmill garbage — turned out to be the perfect insulation. A horse-drawn plow cut ice into uniform blocks you could price and stack like flour. He gave bartenders free ice for a year to get their customers hooked. By 1833 he was shipping ice sixteen thousand miles to Calcutta and undercutting the local supply.

**3/** Then his industry became the incumbent. In 1850 a Florida doctor made ice with a machine and served it in champagne buckets on Bastille Day. His backer died, nobody else would fund him, a New York paper called him a crank, and he died broke in 1855. Forty years later Southern ice factories were naming themselves after him.

**4/** The machine finally worked when a Munich professor made a compressor that didn't leak — for a brewery. Then a Cape Cod butcher's apprentice in Chicago stopped shipping the cow and shipped the meat. Every railroad refused to build his refrigerator cars. He built his own. The butchers of twenty states fought it to the Supreme Court and lost, nine to nothing.

**5/** Bringing the machine into the kitchen meant a fluid that wouldn't kill you when it leaked. In 1929 a Chicago family of three died in their sleep from one. Einstein and Szilárd designed a fridge with no moving parts — the prototype "howled like a jackal." They lost to a GM chemist who found a gas that couldn't burn or poison you, and blew out a candle with it.

**6/** That gas was Freon. It passed every test the engineers wrote. It failed the one nobody had written: don't last a century and reach the sky. A postdoc who'd quit laser chemistry over its weapons use asked where the molecules went. A British program about to be defunded found the hole over Antarctica. Two years later: a treaty. The company that made the gas reversed itself in twenty days.

**7/** Today roughly three-quarters of what an American eats has passed through cold. A billion people are still where New York's tenements were in 1900. And the machine in your kitchen has been through four fluids in a hundred years — every one of them the safe one, the day it was chosen. Backbone, episode one. [LINK]

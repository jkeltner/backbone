--- SEGMENT BREAK: Built In - The Big Picture ---

JEFF: Okay. We've been at this for a while now.

CYRUS: Yeah we have. Two hundred years of cold.

JEFF: Two hundred years of cold. And I want to just... step back for a second. Because I think if you've been following along with us, you'll see something that's really hard to see when you're in the middle of any one of these waves.

CYRUS: What's that?

JEFF: Every single wave ended with somebody getting destroyed. Every one. Tudor buries the natural ice guys. The ammonia machines bury Tudor's whole empire. Freon and the sealed compressor bury the ammonia guys. The cold chain buries the local butchers. And at every single moment, the people getting buried were not crazy. They were not stupid. They were often right about the risks — they just couldn't stop what was coming.

CYRUS: And here's the thing that gets me — they often had reasonable arguments. The railroads weren't wrong that Swift's refrigerated cars changed their whole business model. The natural ice guys weren't wrong that mechanical machines were dangerous. They were just on the wrong side of the wave.

JEFF: Right. And now we're sitting inside the world that all of those waves built. So let me just put some numbers on it, because I think they hit differently now than they did when we threw them out at the top of the episode.

CYRUS: Yeah.

JEFF: In eighteen oh six, shipping frozen pond water to the tropics was a joke. The Boston Gazette literally called it a "slippery speculation." In eighteen fifty-five, the man who invented the underlying principle behind every single refrigerator on Earth died broke and forgotten at fifty-one.

CYRUS: Gorrie. Completely humiliated.

JEFF: Completely humiliated. In nineteen eleven, eating year-old cold-stored food required — and I love this phrase — "considerable courage." In nineteen thirty, eight percent of American homes had a refrigerator. By nineteen forty-four, eighty-five percent did.

CYRUS: Fourteen years. Eight percent to eighty-five percent.

JEFF: That's Freon. That's what happens when you remove the toxicity barrier. And today — today — seventy percent of all food on Earth passes through the cold chain at some point. Refrigeration consumes twenty-five to thirty percent of global electricity. Hundreds of millions of people live in climates that are only habitable because of air conditioning.

CYRUS: We have built a world that physically cannot function at ambient temperature.

JEFF: That's exactly right. And we built it in two centuries. Starting with a twenty-three year old from Boston who couldn't get anyone to take his ship full of ice seriously.

CYRUS: [pause] That is quite an arc.

--- SEGMENT BREAK: Built In - Backbone Test ---

JEFF: So that brings us to the Backbone Test — the five questions we ask about every technology when it crosses from "remarkable" to "built in." And I have to say, refrigeration is maybe the cleanest answer to every single one of these questions I can imagine.

CYRUS: Let's find out. Number one: is it invisible?

JEFF: [laughs] Completely invisible. Like, think about the last time you thought about your refrigerator. Not when you opened it to get food out — that's just automatic at this point. I mean actually thought about it as a piece of technology.

CYRUS: I genuinely cannot remember.

JEFF: No one can. It crossed from "remarkable" to "assumed" sometime in the nineteen-fifties, when the last icemen retired and refrigerators stopped being advertised as miracles and started being sold as... kitchen furniture. Another box in the kitchen. As forgettable as the sink.

CYRUS: And that's the tell, right? The technology disappears into the background. You only notice infrastructure when it fails.

JEFF: Which brings me to something I want to talk about, because this is the best modern illustration of what happens when this invisible infrastructure goes away. February twenty twenty-one. Texas. Winter Storm Uri.

CYRUS: Oh wow.

JEFF: So Winter Storm Uri hits Texas with temperatures nobody there had planned for. And the power grid — and I want to be clear, that is a whole separate story that someone should tell, it's a story about grid design and deregulation — goes down. Four point eight million customers lose power, some of them for more than a week.

CYRUS: Right. And when the power goes down—

JEFF: Everything goes down. The grocery stores lose power. H-E-B — Texas's dominant grocery chain, and honestly one of the best-run supply chains in the country — H-E-B issues a statement saying there has been "severe disruption in the food supply chain" because of "unprecedented weather." Which is a very restrained way to say: everything is going wrong simultaneously.

CYRUS: What are people actually doing at that point?

JEFF: This is the part that gets me. People started storing their remaining food in coolers packed with snow outside their homes. Because the machines had stopped, so they went back to the original method — using nature's cold.

CYRUS: Wait. Is that right? They're literally doing Tudor's ice trade, but in reverse?

JEFF: In reverse, in their own front yards, in twenty twenty-one. The most technologically advanced state in the most technologically advanced country in the world was reduced, for a week, to storing food in snowbanks. One Kroger in Houston — people lined up at four in the morning. The store was sold out of bread, milk, cheese, chicken, and water by afternoon. A major chicken supplier shut down entirely. Farmers were dumping milk because the processing plants had no power.

CYRUS: How many people died?

JEFF: At least two hundred forty-six. Some estimates put it closer to seven hundred. Mostly hypothermia, but the point for us is what the collapse of the cold chain looked like. Eighty billion to a hundred and thirty billion dollars in economic damage. The costliest natural disaster in Texas history. Agricultural losses alone: over six hundred million dollars.

CYRUS: In a week.

JEFF: In a week. That's what invisible infrastructure looks like when it becomes visible. And listeners — I want you to sit with that for a second. Because when we talk about refrigeration being "built in," this is what that actually means. Not built into the technology. Built into civilization. Built into the assumption that cold is always available.

CYRUS: Okay. Question two: what depends on it?

JEFF: Everything.

CYRUS: [laughs] That's your whole answer?

JEFF: I mean... yes? Let me put it this way. Seventy percent of all food we eat. Essentially all vaccines — the WHO estimates that up to fifty percent of vaccines are wasted globally due to cold-chain failures, not just COVID, but all vaccines. Insulin, blood products, organ transplants. Semiconductor manufacturing. Data centers — the internet, AI, all of it requires massive cooling systems to function.

CYRUS: And then there's the political map.

JEFF: And then there's the political map. This is the one I find genuinely astonishing when I really think about it. Willis Carrier invents modern air conditioning in nineteen oh two. By the nineteen-fifties, affordable window units start showing up. And what happens?

CYRUS: Phoenix.

JEFF: Phoenix. Sixty-five thousand people in nineteen forty. Four hundred thirty-nine thousand in nineteen sixty. A four-hundred-seventy-five percent increase in twenty years. Maricopa County — the broader metro area — goes from a hundred and eighty-six thousand in nineteen forty to one point five million in nineteen eighty. Seven hundred eleven percent growth.

CYRUS: Because of air conditioning.

JEFF: Without air conditioning, Phoenix in July is not a city. It's a punishment. From nineteen sixty to two thousand, Sun Belt states gained eighty-six seats in the House of Representatives and the Electoral College. Eighty-six seats. The political map of the United States was redrawn by a technology that moves heat from one place to another.

CYRUS: That doesn't get taught in civics class.

JEFF: It absolutely does not. I think it should be. The same physics that keeps your food from spoiling is why American politics looks the way it does. And here's the version of this I find most striking: the COVID vaccine cold chain. The Pfizer-BioNTech mRNA vaccine required storage at minus seventy degrees Celsius. Colder than the South Pole. Colder than winter in Antarctica.

CYRUS: Which, if you have the infrastructure, is technically doable. But if you don't—

JEFF: If you don't, you have Peru, where only thirty ultra-cold freezers existed in the entire country. All thirty of them in Lima. None anywhere else. In Sub-Saharan Africa, one in four health facilities had no electricity at all. One in four. And in the United States — which does have the infrastructure — we still discarded eighty-two million vaccine doses between December twenty twenty and June twenty twenty-one. One point one billion dollars of vaccines. Gone.

CYRUS: Holy cow.

JEFF: And that's us. That's the country that invented the cold chain. So the dependency is massive, and the gap between who has the infrastructure and who doesn't is itself one of the biggest public health crises in the world.

CYRUS: Okay. Question three: what's the hidden cost? And I want to go here, because this is where I think we actually disagree.

JEFF: [laughs] Let's find out.

CYRUS: So here's my version. The hidden cost is not just any particular refrigerant. It's the structure of the problem. We need more cooling because the planet is warming. But cooling consumes energy that accelerates warming. And here is the number that I cannot get out of my head: some projections have global cooling demand surging more than thirty-fold by two thousand one hundred — the IEA projects a tripling just by twenty-fifty, and some regional models for the hottest parts of the world go much higher.

JEFF: Thirty-three times?

CYRUS: Thirty-three times. Because billions of people in the hottest climates on Earth — India, Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa — are gaining access to cooling for the first time. India's air conditioner sales are projected to grow six-fold by twenty thirty-eight. Six times. That's not a rounding error.

JEFF: That's not a rounding error.

CYRUS: And here's the structural problem. The people who most need cooling are the ones in the hottest, often poorest places. And their adoption of cooling is going to be the thing that most accelerates the warming that makes them need more cooling. There's a feedback loop here that I don't see an institutional solution to. The incentives point directly against solving it.

JEFF: Yeah, and I think... I mean, I hear that. And I'm not sure it is as structurally unsolvable as you're making it sound. Here's the thing: we've actually done this before. The Montreal Protocol — when the ozone crisis hit — that was international cooperation working. That was governments agreeing to eliminate CFCs across the board, and it worked. The ozone layer is recovering.

CYRUS: A hundred percent. The Montreal Protocol worked. But here's what happened. We replaced CFCs with HFCs. And HFCs turned out to have a global warming potential up to fourteen thousand eight hundred times that of CO2. So we fixed the chemistry and created the next problem.

JEFF: Which is why the Kigali Amendment in twenty sixteen is supposed to address HFCs. And if it gets fully implemented, the modeling says it could avoid zero point four degrees Celsius of warming by two thousand one hundred.

CYRUS: If.

JEFF: If. But that's not nothing. Zero point four degrees is actually a meaningful number.

CYRUS: It is. But look — I'm not saying institutions can't do anything. I'm saying the structural problem is bigger than any single amendment can solve. Cooling consumes twenty-five to thirty percent of global electricity right now. And demand is about to explode. The Kigali Amendment is not going to bend that curve on its own.

JEFF: I think where I land on this is — yes, it's structural, but I don't think that means there's no institutional lever. The Kigali Amendment matters. The UNICEF solar refrigerators matter. The Einstein fridge revival matters. These aren't sufficient, but they're not nothing.

CYRUS: Right. I'm not saying do nothing. I'm saying don't kid yourself that the institutional fix is going to be enough. The scale of the demand shift is going to require a technological leap, not just better policy.

JEFF: I think that's probably right. The way I think about it is — policy buys time. Technology has to close the gap.

CYRUS: That I can agree with.

JEFF: And I want to come back to something I said in the last wave, because I promised we'd return to it — the housework paradox. The refrigerator was marketed as liberating women from daily shopping and daily spoilage. And it did do that. But what actually happened is that the expectation ratcheted up. Weekly shopping replaced daily shopping, yes — but now dinner was supposed to have more variety, more courses, higher standards. The time saved by the refrigerator got absorbed by rising expectations for what the kitchen was supposed to produce. And that's the same dynamic you see with email. Email was supposed to save time. What it created was the expectation that you respond to everything within hours. The technology that removes friction creates the standard that fills the friction back in.

CYRUS: The refrigerator didn't liberate the kitchen. It raised the bar for what the kitchen was supposed to be.

JEFF: Right. And that's a hidden cost that is completely invisible until you're looking for it. It's not on anyone's balance sheet. But the social reorganization of domestic labor around refrigeration is real. Anyway. The hidden cost also isn't just climate. There's something Nicola Twilley points out in her book Frostbite — refrigeration reduced our exposure to fermented foods, to the beneficial microbes in food that was naturally preserved in the pre-refrigeration era. We've built an economy premised on cold equaling fresh, and it's not clear that assumption is even scientifically correct. Flash-frozen food is often fresher than refrigerated food that's been sitting in your fridge for four days.

CYRUS: "Fresh, never frozen." That's still a marketing premium.

JEFF: It's a marketing premium that goes back to the nineteen eleven Cold-Storage Banquet. Where they literally tried to prove that a year-old egg was fine to eat. We've been arguing about this for a hundred and fifteen years.

CYRUS: [laughs] We have not resolved it.

JEFF: We have not resolved it. Okay. Question four: could we go back?

CYRUS: No.

JEFF: That's it?

CYRUS: [laughs] I mean, what do you want me to say? Seven billion people are fed through the cold chain. Hundreds of millions live in climates that are only habitable because of air conditioning. Vaccines, insulin, organ transplants — all require cold. The global food system would collapse within a week without refrigeration. We are not going back.

JEFF: And here's the thing I keep coming back to — Tudor's whole business model was built on creating dependency. That was his insight. You had to get people to want cold before they would pay for cold. He didn't just build a business; he manufactured a human expectation. And that expectation, two hundred years later, is so deep that it's physically load-bearing. We built cities on it. We built a food supply for seven billion people on it. Tudor's question from eighteen oh six — can you make people depend on cold? — has been answered. You can. And now you cannot take it back.

CYRUS: The dependency is total.

JEFF: The dependency is total. Okay — question five. What's next?

CYRUS: So this is where I actually get genuinely excited, because a couple of things are happening simultaneously. The first is the Kigali Amendment doing its work on HFCs — targeting the refrigerants that replaced the ozone-destroying ones. If it gets fully implemented, that zero point four degrees matters.

JEFF: And natural refrigerants are making a comeback. Ammonia, CO2, hydrocarbons — all of them are being revisited, partly because they don't have the HFC problem and partly because the regulatory environment is starting to push in that direction. It's a bit of an ironic echo of the pre-Freon era.

CYRUS: And then there's Einstein's fridge.

JEFF: Which — and I will just say this — is one of the great buried stories in this whole episode. Oxford University engineers have actually revived Einstein and Szilard's nineteen thirty design — the refrigerator with no moving parts, no toxic refrigerants, no electricity required. The one Einstein patented after reading about a family killed by refrigerant fumes leaking from their icebox.

CYRUS: That design might finally find its purpose — a century later.

JEFF: A century later. For off-grid developing-world use. Which is exactly the use case Einstein and Szilard had in mind when they designed it. UNICEF is also deploying solar-powered vaccine refrigerators in rural areas without electricity — leapfrogging the grid entirely. So there's real innovation happening.

CYRUS: But the fundamental challenge — how do you provide universal access to cold without making the planet uninhabitable — that is still the open question. And here's the thing: that is actually the same question John Gorrie was trying to answer in eighteen forty-four. He wanted to cool sickrooms to save lives in a warming climate.

JEFF: And we're still working on it.

CYRUS: We're still working on it. A hundred and eighty years later.

CYRUS: First one's mine, and it's the cooling paradox. I keep coming back to this because it is genuinely structurally alarming. We need more cooling because the planet is warming. Cooling accelerates warming. The people who most need cooling are in the places that are warming fastest and have the least infrastructure to handle it. And a potential thirty-fold-plus surge in cooling demand by two thousand one hundred is not a solved problem.

JEFF: And the specific version of this that I think is worth sitting with: India. India is projected to grow air conditioner sales six-fold by twenty thirty-eight. Six times. That's not a fringe scenario — that's the base case. And HFCs — the refrigerants currently in use — have that fourteen-thousand-eight-hundred-times-CO2 warming potential. The math on this is not comfortable.

CYRUS: The honest answer is I don't know how you thread that needle without a genuine technology breakthrough. Better efficiency standards help. The Kigali Amendment helps. But none of that bends the curve enough at the scale India alone represents, let alone the full developing world. This is the one where I think we are genuinely racing against ourselves.

JEFF: Second question is mine. The cold-chain gap. Thirteen percent of all food produced globally is lost to inadequate refrigeration. Enough to feed nearly a billion people. Fifty percent of vaccines wasted globally due to cold-chain failures. These numbers are not natural disasters — they're infrastructure deficits. And the infrastructure deficit is solvable.

CYRUS: In principle.

JEFF: In principle. The tools exist. Solar-powered cold storage. Einstein's fridge for off-grid communities. UNICEF's vaccine refrigerators. The question is scale and will — both of which are genuine political and institutional challenges, not physics problems.

CYRUS: The Peru example is brutal. Thirty ultra-cold freezers in the whole country, all in one city, during a global pandemic that required ultra-cold distribution. That's not a technology problem. That's an investment problem.

JEFF: It's a will problem. And I think it's worth naming that the cold-chain gap is the same gap Tudor identified in eighteen twenty when he showed up in Martinique with a ship full of ice and nobody there knew what to do with it. Creating demand in places that don't have the infrastructure for it. It's the same problem, two hundred years later, at vastly higher stakes.

CYRUS: The freshness illusion question — we're going to just briefly flag this one.

JEFF: Yeah. We built an entire food economy on the idea that cold equals fresh. And Nicola Twilley's argument is that it's not quite right — that flash-frozen food is often genuinely fresher than refrigerated food that's been sitting in cold storage for days or weeks. "Fresh, never frozen" is a marketing line. That distinction matters more than we're letting on.

CYRUS: "Fresh, never frozen" was also the argument at the nineteen eleven Cold-Storage Banquet. And it worked. The fear of old food has been with us for a long time, even as refrigeration made us dependent on old food marketed as fresh.

JEFF: This is the worst it will ever be — I actually think that applies here. Our ability to understand what "fresh" actually means scientifically is getting better. The marketing claims are going to start running into actual data.

--- SEGMENT BREAK: Built In - What the Story Teaches ---

JEFF: Okay. The part of this I want to leave people with — and this is the one portable idea from this whole story.

CYRUS: Go.

JEFF: Every wave of refrigeration's history required a new chemistry. And every chemistry solution created a problem its inventors couldn't see. Gorrie's design used the right principle but technology his era couldn't make safe. Linde's ammonia machines were safe for breweries, deadly for home kitchens. Midgley's Freon was safe for home kitchens, catastrophic for the ozone layer. HFCs replaced CFCs and turned out to be potent greenhouse gases.

CYRUS: Every solution is also the next problem.

JEFF: But the specific version that I think is the takeaway — and I want to be careful here, because I don't want this to collapse into "all technology has unintended consequences," which is true but not useful. The specific thing that happened every time is this: the cost only appeared after the solution became load-bearing. By the time we understood what CFCs were doing to the ozone layer, half the civilized world was running on them. By the time Rowland and Molina published their findings in nineteen seventy-four — the findings that won them the Nobel Prize — the dependency was already so deep that the Montreal Protocol took another thirteen years to sign.

CYRUS: Thirteen years. And in those thirteen years, the ozone hole was forming.

JEFF: The ozone hole was forming. And the question I keep sitting with — and this is the one I want to leave listeners with — is not "does this technology work?" Because refrigeration worked. It worked at every stage. The question is: what will we discover about this in forty years, when it's too deeply embedded to remove?

CYRUS: Right. And you can apply that to basically anything we're building right now.

JEFF: You can. I think specifically about AI, because that's where we spend a lot of our time. The things we don't know about long-term AI deployment at scale — the social effects, the economic effects, the concentration effects — those costs are going to appear on the same timeline as refrigeration's hidden costs. After the dependency is load-bearing. Not before.

CYRUS: And by that point, you can't uninvent it. You can only invent around it. The same way we're inventing around HFCs now with natural refrigerants and the Kigali Amendment.

JEFF: This is specifically the thing that the refrigeration story teaches that other technology stories often obscure. The question is not whether the risk is real. The resisters in every wave of refrigeration had real arguments. Gorrie's machine was genuinely dangerous. Ammonia machines did kill people. The cold-storage food did make people genuinely uncomfortable for very reasonable reasons. The resisters were often right about the immediate problem. They just couldn't stop the dependency once it formed. And neither can we.

CYRUS: So the lesson is... don't resist?

JEFF: [pause] No. I think the lesson is: be honest about the timing gap. We talk about technology risk like risk assessment and technology adoption happen at the same speed. They don't. The technology spreads. The risk becomes clear later. The Montreal Protocol is actually the hopeful version of this story — humanity identified the risk before the damage was irreversible, coordinated, and acted. Thirteen years after the science. That's not fast. But it was fast enough for the ozone layer to recover.

CYRUS: The hopeful version.

JEFF: The hopeful version. The less hopeful version is the one we're living with now — a cooling paradox where the solution is also the accelerant. And we built it. We chose it. Every one of us chose it, every time we bought a refrigerator or moved to Phoenix or took a vaccine that needed to be kept at minus seventy.

CYRUS: Tudor's legacy.

JEFF: Tudor's legacy. Cold, built in. [pause] You cannot unring that bell.

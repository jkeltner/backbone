Refrigeration consumes twenty-five to thirty percent of global electricity. Hundreds of millions of people live in climates that are only habitable because of air conditioning.

CYRUS: We have built a world that physically cannot function at ambient temperature.

JEFF: That's exactly right. And we built it in two centuries. Starting with a twenty-three year old from Boston who couldn't get anyone to take his ship full of ice seriously.

CYRUS: [pause] That is quite an arc.

JEFF: So that brings us to the Backbone Test — the five questions we ask about every technology when it crosses from "remarkable" to "built in." And I have to say, refrigeration is maybe the cleanest answer to every single one of these questions I can imagine.

CYRUS: Let's find out. Number one: is it invisible?

JEFF: [laughs] Completely invisible. Like, think about the last time you thought about your refrigerator. Not when you opened it to get food out — that's just automatic at this point. I mean actually thought about it as a piece of technology.

CYRUS: I genuinely cannot remember.

JEFF: No one can. It crossed from "remarkable" to "assumed" sometime in the nineteen-fifties, when the last icemen retired and refrigerators stopped being advertised as miracles and started being sold as... kitchen furniture. Another box in the kitchen. As forgettable as the sink.

CYRUS: And that's the tell, right? The technology disappears into the background. You only notice infrastructure when it fails.

JEFF: Which brings me to something I want to talk about, because this is the best modern illustration of what happens when this invisible infrastructure goes away. February twenty twenty-one. Texas. Winter Storm Uri.

CYRUS: Oh wow.

JEFF: So Winter Storm Uri hits Texas with temperatures nobody there had planned for. And the power grid — and I want to be clear, that is a whole separate story that someone should tell, it's a story about grid design and deregulation — goes down. Four point eight million customers lose power, some of them for more than a week.

CYRUS: Right. And when the power goes down—

H-E-B — Texas's dominant grocery chain, and honestly one of the best-run supply chains in the country — H-E-B issues a statement saying there has been "severe disruption in the food supply chain" because of "unprecedented weather." Which is a very restrained way to say: everything is going wrong simultaneously.

CYRUS: What are people actually doing at that point?

JEFF: This is the part that gets me. People started storing their remaining food in coolers packed with snow outside their homes. Because the machines had stopped, so they went back to the original method — using nature's cold.

CYRUS: Wait. Is that right? They're literally doing Tudor's ice trade, but in reverse?

JEFF: In reverse, in their own front yards, in twenty twenty-one. The most technologically advanced state in the most technologically advanced country in the world was reduced, for a week, to storing food in snowbanks. One Kroger in Houston — people lined up at four in the morning. The store was sold out of bread, milk, cheese, chicken, and water by afternoon. A major chicken supplier shut down entirely. Farmers were dumping milk because the processing plants had no power.

CYRUS: How many people died?

JEFF: At least two hundred forty-six. Some estimates put it closer to seven hundred. Mostly hypothermia, but the point for us is what the collapse of the cold chain looked like. Eighty billion to a hundred and thirty billion dollars in economic damage. The costliest natural disaster in Texas history. Agricultural losses alone: over six hundred million dollars.

CYRUS: In a week.

JEFF: In a week. That's what invisible infrastructure looks like when it becomes visible. And listeners — I want you to sit with that for a second. Because when we talk about refrigeration being "built in," this is what that actually means. Not built into the technology. Built into civilization. Built into the assumption that cold is always available.

CYRUS: Okay. Question two: what depends on it?

JEFF: Everything.

CYRUS: [laughs] That's your whole answer?

JEFF: I mean... yes? Let me put it this way. Seventy percent of all food we eat. Essentially all vaccines — the WHO estimates that up to fifty percent of vaccines are wasted globally due to cold-chain failures, not just COVID, but all vaccines. CYRUS: And then there's the political map.

Maricopa County — the broader metro area — goes from a hundred and eighty-six thousand in nineteen forty to one point five million in nineteen eighty. Seven hundred eleven percent growth.

CYRUS: Because of air conditioning.

JEFF: Without air conditioning, Phoenix in July is not a city. It's a punishment. From nineteen sixty to two thousand, Sun Belt states gained eighty-six seats in the House of Representatives and the Electoral College. Eighty-six seats. The political map of the United States was redrawn by a technology that moves heat from one place to another.

CYRUS: That doesn't get taught in civics class.

JEFF: It absolutely does not. I think it should be. The same physics that keeps your food from spoiling is why American politics looks the way it does. And here's the version of this I find most striking: the COVID vaccine cold chain. The Pfizer-BioNTech mRNA vaccine required storage at minus seventy degrees Celsius. Colder than the South Pole. Colder than winter in Antarctica.

CYRUS: Which, if you have the infrastructure, is technically doable. But if you don't—

JEFF: If you don't, you have Peru, where only thirty ultra-cold freezers existed in the entire country. All thirty of them in Lima. None anywhere else. In Sub-Saharan Africa, one in four health facilities had no electricity at all. One in four. And in the United States — which does have the infrastructure — we still discarded eighty-two million vaccine doses between December twenty twenty and June twenty twenty-one. One point one billion dollars of vaccines. Gone.

CYRUS: Holy cow.

JEFF: And that's us. That's the country that invented the cold chain. So the dependency is massive, and the gap between who has the infrastructure and who doesn't is itself one of the biggest public health crises in the world.

And here is the number that I cannot get out of my head: some projections have global cooling demand surging more than thirty-fold by two thousand one hundred — the IEA projects a tripling just by twenty-fifty, and some regional models for the hottest parts of the world go much higher.

JEFF: Thirty-three times?

CYRUS: Thirty-three times. Because billions of people in the hottest climates on Earth — India, Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa — are gaining access to cooling for the first time. JEFF: That's not a rounding error.

The incentives point directly against solving it.

JEFF: Yeah, and I think... I mean, I hear that. And I'm not sure it is as structurally unsolvable as you're making it sound. Here's the thing: we've actually done this before. The ozone layer is recovering.

CYRUS: A hundred percent. The Montreal Protocol worked. But here's what happened. We replaced CFCs with HFCs. And HFCs turned out to have a global warming potential up to fourteen thousand eight hundred times that of CO2. So we fixed the chemistry and created the next problem.

JEFF: Which is why the Kigali Amendment in twenty sixteen is supposed to address HFCs. And if it gets fully implemented, the modeling says it could avoid zero point four degrees Celsius of warming by two thousand one hundred.

JEFF: I think where I land on this is — yes, it's structural, but I don't think that means there's no institutional lever. The Kigali Amendment matters. The UNICEF solar refrigerators matter. The Einstein fridge revival matters. These aren't sufficient, but they're not nothing.

CYRUS: Right. I'm not saying do nothing. I'm saying don't kid yourself that the institutional fix is going to be enough. The scale of the demand shift is going to require a technological leap, not just better policy.

JEFF: I think that's probably right. The way I think about it is — policy buys time. Technology has to close the gap.

CYRUS: That I can agree with.

JEFF: And I want to come back to something I said in the last wave, because I promised we'd return to it — the housework paradox. The refrigerator was marketed as liberating women from daily shopping and daily spoilage. And it did do that. But what actually happened is that the expectation ratcheted up. Weekly shopping replaced daily shopping, yes — but now dinner was supposed to have more variety, more courses, higher standards. The time saved by the refrigerator got absorbed by rising expectations for what the kitchen was supposed to produce. And that's the same dynamic you see with email. Email was supposed to save time. What it created was the expectation that you respond to everything within hours. The technology that removes friction creates the standard that fills the friction back in.

CYRUS: The refrigerator didn't liberate the kitchen. It raised the bar for what the kitchen was supposed to be.

JEFF: Right. And that's a hidden cost that is completely invisible until you're looking for it. It's not on anyone's balance sheet. But the social reorganization of domestic labor around refrigeration is real. Anyway. The hidden cost also isn't just climate. There's something Nicola Twilley points out in her book Frostbite — refrigeration reduced our exposure to fermented foods, to the beneficial microbes in food that was naturally preserved in the pre-refrigeration era. We've built an economy premised on cold equaling fresh, and it's not clear that assumption is even scientifically correct. Flash-frozen food is often fresher than refrigerated food that's been sitting in your fridge for four days.

CYRUS: "Fresh, never frozen." That's still a marketing premium.

JEFF: It's a marketing premium that goes back to the nineteen eleven Cold-Storage Banquet. Where they literally tried to prove that a year-old egg was fine to eat. We've been arguing about this for a hundred and fifteen years.

CYRUS: [laughs] We have not resolved it.

CYRUS: No.

CYRUS: [laughs] I mean, what do you want me to say? Seven billion people are fed through the cold chain. Hundreds of millions live in climates that are only habitable because of air conditioning. JEFF: And here's the thing I keep coming back to — Tudor's whole business model was built on creating dependency. That was his insight. You had to get people to want cold before they would pay for cold. He didn't just build a business; he manufactured a human expectation. And that expectation, two hundred years later, is so deep that it's physically load-bearing. We built cities on it. We built a food supply for seven billion people on it. Tudor's question from eighteen oh six — can you make people depend on cold? — has been answered. You can. And now you cannot take it back.

CYRUS: The dependency is total.

JEFF: The dependency is total. Okay — question five. What's next?

CYRUS: So this is where I actually get genuinely excited, because a couple of things are happening simultaneously. By the time we understood what CFCs were doing to the ozone layer, half the civilized world was running on them. CYRUS: Right. And you can apply that to basically anything we're building right now.
